Why can something inside the electron move slower than light, when the equation that describes the electron is built on the speed of light?

This short paper is part of the 0-Sphere Model, a research programme that pictures the electron not as a structureless point but as two tiny "kernels" with energy passing back and forth between them. From one measured number, the electron's anomalous magnetic moment (a small correction to its magnetic strength), the model fixes the speed of this internal transfer without any adjustable parameter: about 4 percent of the speed of light (0.04047 c). That speed gives the electron an internal rhythm of about 5 billion billion oscillations per second, which corresponds to X-rays with an energy of about 20.7 keV.

This raises an obvious objection. The Dirac equation, the standard equation for the electron, is built on the speed of light, and it predicts a rapid trembling of the electron (called "Zitterbewegung", German for "trembling motion") at the speed of light, with a frequency about 50 times higher. How can the model's 4 percent be right?

The paper's answer is that the phrase "the speed of light" is used in two different senses. The first is the universal speed limit of space and time, the c of Einstein's relativity, which is the same for every observer. The second is the speed at which a signal travels through a material. Light in water moves at about three quarters of c, and nobody thinks relativity breaks down in a glass of water. The paper keeps the first meaning exactly as it is and applies the second one inside the electron: the energy carried between the two kernels behaves as if it were moving through a medium with a refractive index of about 25.

Two well-known results support this reading. First, Fizeau's experiment of 1851 measured light in flowing water and found that it obeys relativity perfectly, even though it travels more slowly than c. Second, a standard rewriting of the Dirac equation, due to Foldy and Wouthuysen, shows that the "speed of light" trembling depends on how one chooses to define the electron's position. So it does not tell us the speed of anything inside the electron.

The paper also explains why it speaks of a "group velocity", the speed of a wave packet rather than of individual wave crests. In the model, energy never jumps from one kernel to the other all at once. It moves gradually: the amount in transit starts at zero, is largest halfway through, and returns to zero. A transfer with a beginning and an end is a short wave packet, and a wave packet carries energy at its group velocity. Using only the trigonometric identities taught in high school, the paper shows that the energy held at each kernel contains exactly three frequencies and nothing in between. The transfer is smooth when followed in time, yet built from a few sharp frequencies.

The paper is careful about its limits. The refractive index is assigned from the measured magnetic moment, not yet derived from deeper principles. A factor of two between the two predicted frequencies is not explained. And the internal speed has never been measured. The two theories therefore make different predictions for the same phenomenon, and the paper names the test: a search for an X-ray signal of the free electron at about 20 keV.
