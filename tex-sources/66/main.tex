\begin{abstract}
The 0-Sphere model assigns to the electron an internal oscillation whose speed, fixed by the anomalous magnetic moment alone, is $v_{\text{ZB}} = 0.04047\,c$, giving a Zitterbewegung frequency $\nu_{\text{ZB}} = v_{\text{ZB}}/\lambda_C = 5.00\times10^{18}$~Hz in the X-ray band.
The Dirac equation attaches the speed $c$ to the Zitterbewegung through the eigenvalues $\pm c$ of its velocity operator and predicts $2m_ec^2/h = 2.47\times10^{20}$~Hz.
This Letter separates two meanings of $c$ that the comparison conflates: the invariant speed of the Lorentz group, which the 0-Sphere model retains without modification, and the group velocity of the internal carrier, which it sets to $c/n$ with $n = 1/\beta_{\text{ZB}} = 24.71$.
Fizeau's moving-water experiment shows that a medium-internal speed $c/n$ is compatible with special relativity; here the medium rest frame is the electron rest frame, and the Fresnel drag coefficient is $1-\beta_{\text{ZB}}^2$.
The Foldy--Wouthuysen representation shows that the eigenvalues $\pm c$ belong to one choice of position operator and do not fix the carrier speed as an observable.
The optical metric $\mathcal{G} = n^2(T)\,g$ established earlier in the series supplies the geometric setting, and Snell's law gives a critical angle $\sin\theta_c = \beta_{\text{ZB}}$ at any boundary where the index returns to unity, independently of the shape of that boundary.
The index is assigned, not derived; its derivation from the internal temperature profile is identified as the route by which the bridge equation becomes a derivation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The Zitterbewegung (ZB) of the free Dirac electron was identified by Schr\"odinger~\cite{Schrodinger1930} as an interference between positive- and negative-energy components. Its angular frequency is set by the energy gap $2m_ec^2$, and its amplitude is of order $\hbar/(2m_ec)$. Breit~\cite{Breit1928} had already noted that the velocity operator of the Dirac equation~\cite{Dirac1928},
\begin{equation}
\dot{\bm{x}} = \frac{i}{\hbar}[H_D,\bm{x}] = c\,\bm{\alpha},
\label{eq:velop}
\end{equation}
has eigenvalues $\pm c$ along any axis. In the standard reading, the ZB is therefore a trembling motion at the speed of light with frequency
\begin{equation}
\nu_{\text{D}} = \frac{2m_ec^2}{h} = 2.4712\times10^{20}\ \text{Hz}.
\label{eq:nuD}
\end{equation}

The 0-Sphere model reaches a different number by a different route. In this model the electron consists of two kernels, $A$ and $B$, separated by $\lambda_C/2$, and an amount of energy, the photon sphere, that is carried back and forth between them (Appendix~\ref{app:model} and Fig.~\ref{fig:model}). Paper \#10~\cite{H10} fixes the speed of that transport from the anomalous magnetic moment alone, with no free parameter,
\begin{equation}
\beta_{\text{ZB}}^2 = 0.00163798087, \qquad \beta_{\text{ZB}} = 0.040472.
\label{eq:beta}
\end{equation}
One cycle of the internal phase is one round trip of the photon sphere from $A$ to $B$ and back, a path of length $\lambda_C = h/(m_ec)$~\cite{H51}. The frequency is then
\begin{equation}
\nu_{\text{ZB}} = \frac{\beta_{\text{ZB}}\,c}{\lambda_C} = \frac{\beta_{\text{ZB}}\,m_ec^2}{h} = 5.0007\times10^{18}\ \text{Hz},
\label{eq:nuZB}
\end{equation}
corresponding to a photon energy $\beta_{\text{ZB}}m_ec^2 = 20.7$~keV. The ratio $\nu_{\text{D}}/\nu_{\text{ZB}} = 2/\beta_{\text{ZB}} = 49.42$. Earlier statements of the 0-Sphere frequency and of its ratio to the Dirac value, in Appendix~G of Paper \#33~\cite{H33} and in Paper \#46~\cite{H46}, contain an arithmetic error and are superseded by Eq.~(\ref{eq:nuZB}).

The two predictions are in direct competition, and the natural objection is immediate: the Dirac equation employs $c$, and the ZB it produces moves at $c$, so an internal motion at $0.04\,c$ appears to contradict the equation from which the electron's spin and $g=2$ are obtained. This Letter answers the objection by separating two statements that the objection treats as one.

\paragraph{Scope.} The Letter takes two inputs from the series: the value~(\ref{eq:beta}) of Paper \#10 and the optical metric of Paper \#51. It delivers three outputs: the separation of the invariant speed from the carrier speed (Secs.~\ref{sec:two}--\ref{sec:fw}), the assignment of an internal refractive index $n = 1/\beta_{\text{ZB}}$ within that metric (Sec.~\ref{sec:optical}), and the consequences of that index that do not depend on the shape of the internal region (Sec.~\ref{sec:snell}). Three questions are outside its scope: the shape and structure of the boundary of the internal region, the rate at which energy leaks across it, and the coupling of the internal carrier to gravitation. The origin of the internal oscillation is not addressed either; the energy identity of the model describes its persistence, not its beginning. Appendix~\ref{app:model} summarizes the elements of the 0-Sphere model on which the argument rests, Appendix~\ref{app:why} sets out why a group velocity belongs in this model and which questions it opens, Appendix~\ref{app:numbers} reproduces every number with its formula, and Appendix~\ref{app:interface} lists the inputs, results and open items in a form that later papers of the series can cite directly.

\section{Two Meanings of $c$}
\label{sec:two}

Light that enters a block of glass travels at about two thirds of $c$, and light in water at $c/1.33$. No one concludes from this that special relativity fails inside glass or water. The speed of a signal inside a medium is a property of the medium; the speed $c$ that appears in the Lorentz transformation is a property of spacetime, and it is the same inside the glass as outside. The argument of this Letter is that the speed $0.04\,c$ of the 0-Sphere model belongs to the first kind and the speed $c$ of the Dirac equation to the second, so that the two do not compete.

\paragraph{Invariant speed.} In the Dirac equation, $c$ enters through the requirement of Lorentz covariance. It is the invariant speed of the Lorentz group, the parameter that appears in every boost and in the dispersion relation $E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4$. It is a property of spacetime, not of any particular signal.

\paragraph{Carrier speed.} The 0-Sphere model describes the electron as two kernels connected by an internal carrier, the photon sphere, that travels back and forth between them~\cite{H33,H51}. The speed of that carrier is a property of the internal structure. Nothing in Lorentz covariance requires it to equal the invariant speed, just as nothing in Lorentz covariance requires light in glass to travel at $c$.

\paragraph{Position of this Letter.} The 0-Sphere model retains the invariant speed $c$ without modification. The quantity it revises is the group velocity of the internal carrier,
\begin{equation}
v_{\text{ZB}} = \frac{c}{n}, \qquad n = \frac{1}{\beta_{\text{ZB}}} = 24.708.
\label{eq:n}
\end{equation}
The statement that the internal carrier does not travel at $c$ is accordingly to be read in the sense of a refractive medium: the signal speed limit and the invariant speed are untouched, and only the propagation of the internal carrier is slowed.

\paragraph{Why a group velocity.} A wave of a single frequency that has neither beginning nor end has a phase velocity but no group velocity; a group velocity exists only for a superposition of frequencies, that is, for a wave of finite duration. In the 0-Sphere model the energy does not jump from kernel $A$ to kernel $B$. It passes gradually, through the internal phase: the share held at $A$ falls while the share held at $B$ rises, and the energy in transit rises from zero to a maximum and falls back to zero (Appendix~\ref{app:split}). Each transfer is therefore a wave group of finite duration, and it is the group velocity of that wave group that Eq.~(\ref{eq:n}) refers to (Fig.~\ref{fig:group} and Appendix~\ref{app:why}). The shape of the wave group is not chosen: it is fixed by the energy identity of the model, and its amplitude is not constant over the transfer (Appendix~\ref{app:envelope}).

\section{Precedent: Light in a Moving Medium}
\label{sec:fizeau}

In 1851 Fizeau~\cite{Fizeau1851} measured the speed of light in water flowing at speed $v$. Light sent along the flow travels faster than light sent against it, but not by the full amount $v$: the water drags the light only partially. The result is reproduced by the relativistic composition of velocities~\cite{Laue1907},
\begin{align}
u &= \frac{c/n + v}{1 + v/(nc)}
\notag\\
&\simeq \frac{c}{n} + v\left(1 - \frac{1}{n^2}\right),
\label{eq:fizeau}
\end{align}
where the second line holds to first order in $v/c$. The factor $1-1/n^2$ is the Fresnel drag coefficient. Equation~(\ref{eq:fizeau}) contains the essential point: a medium selects a rest frame, the signal travels at $c/n$ in that frame, and every observer still composes velocities with the invariant speed $c$. Special relativity is not violated; it is used.

For the electron the medium is internal, and its rest frame is the electron rest frame. A moving electron is then a moving medium, and its internal carrier is dragged with coefficient
\begin{equation}
1 - \frac{1}{n^2} = 1 - \beta_{\text{ZB}}^2 = 0.998362.
\label{eq:drag}
\end{equation}
The electron as a whole remains a Lorentz-covariant object. The only frame-dependent statement, that the carrier speed equals $c/n$, refers to the frame in which the electron is at rest, exactly as the statement that light in water travels at $c/1.33$ refers to the frame in which the water is at rest. The departure of the drag coefficient from unity lies on the $\beta^2$ layer of the three-layer hierarchy of the model (Appendix~\ref{app:layers}), the layer that carries the anomalous magnetic moment.

\section{The Dirac Eigenvalues Do Not Fix the Carrier Speed}
\label{sec:fw}

The eigenvalues $\pm c$ of Eq.~(\ref{eq:velop}) are a property of the operator $\bm{x}$ in the Dirac representation. Foldy and Wouthuysen~\cite{FW1950} showed that a unitary transformation decouples positive and negative energies, and that the corresponding mean position operator $\bm{X}$ obeys
\begin{equation}
\dot{\bm{X}} = \frac{c^2\bm{p}}{E},
\label{eq:FW}
\end{equation}
which contains no trembling term and whose magnitude is bounded below $c$ for massive states. The same mean position coincides with the Newton--Wigner localized state~\cite{NW1949}. The ZB with speed $c$ is thus attached to one choice of position operator, and Dirac himself explained the eigenvalues $\pm c$ by noting that a measurement of an instantaneous velocity requires measurements of position at two nearly equal times, which leave the momentum, and with it the velocity, almost completely undetermined~\cite{DiracPrinciples}.

The Dirac theory therefore does not determine the speed of an internal carrier as an observable. It determines the gap $2m_ec^2$ and the invariant speed $c$. The 0-Sphere model fills the place that the Dirac theory leaves open, and its value is fixed by a measured quantity, the anomalous magnetic moment, rather than by the spectrum of an operator whose physical status is representation-dependent.

\paragraph{Independence of the two statements.} The water analogy invites the expectation that the vacuum limit $n \to 1$ recovers the Dirac ZB. That expectation is not adopted here. In the bridge equation of Appendix~\ref{app:bridge}, the limit $\beta_{\text{ZB}} \to 1$ sends the Lorentz factor, and with it the anomalous moment, to infinity rather than to the Dirac value $a_e = 0$. The argument of Sec.~\ref{sec:fizeau} (a carrier speed $c/n$ is relativistically admissible) and the argument of the present section (the Dirac eigenvalues $\pm c$ are not a carrier speed) are therefore stated independently, and neither relies on the other.

\section{The Optical Metric of the Internal Region}
\label{sec:optical}

Paper \#24~\cite{H24} introduced thermal geodesics, the paths of internal energy transport determined by the internal temperature distribution. Paper \#51~\cite{H51} established, as an identity rather than an analogy, that the thermal metric is the optical metric of a medium with temperature-dependent refractive index,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{G}_{\mu\nu} = n^2(T)\,g_{\mu\nu},
\label{eq:optical}
\end{equation}
and that thermal geodesics obey the thermal Fermat principle $\delta\!\int\!\kappa\,ds = 0$ (Appendix~\ref{app:optical}). The interpretation of Eq.~(\ref{eq:n}) is therefore not introduced for the present purpose. The geometric framework in which the internal carrier propagates as in a refractive medium is already part of the series, and Eq.~(\ref{eq:n}) assigns the value that the index takes in the internal region:
\begin{equation}
n(T_{\text{int}}) = \frac{c}{v_{\text{ZB}}} = \frac{1}{\beta_{\text{ZB}}}.
\label{eq:nT}
\end{equation}
With $n^2 = 610.51$, the index also satisfies $n^2 \simeq \sqrt{2}\,\pi/\alpha = 608.83$ to $0.28\%$, which restates the first-order relation $\beta_{\text{ZB}} \simeq [\alpha/(\sqrt{2}\pi)]^{1/2}$ of the series. The question why the internal index is about $25$ is the same question as why the internal speed is about $\alpha^{1/2}$.

\section{Shape-Independent Consequences}
\label{sec:snell}

Wherever the index falls from its internal value to unity, Snell's law $n\sin\theta_{\text{in}} = \sin\theta_{\text{out}}$ gives total internal reflection above the critical angle
\begin{equation}
\sin\theta_c = \frac{1}{n} = \beta_{\text{ZB}}, \qquad \theta_c = 2.32^\circ .
\label{eq:thetac}
\end{equation}
For comparison, the critical angle of water is $48.8^\circ$ and that of diamond $24.4^\circ$; an index of $24.7$ returns to the interior almost every ray that meets the boundary. This statement depends only on the two values of the index and not on the shape of the region in which the higher value holds. Together with the drag coefficient~(\ref{eq:drag}) it forms the set of consequences of Eq.~(\ref{eq:n}) that require no further assumption, collected in Table~\ref{tab:layers}. Quantities that do depend on the shape, such as the fraction of the internal flux that escapes per boundary encounter or the degree to which the internal region confines the carrier, require a model of the boundary and are outside the scope of this Letter.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{The index is not derived.} Equation~(\ref{eq:nT}) assigns $n$ from the anomalous magnetic moment through $\beta_{\text{ZB}}$. As it stands, $n$ carries no information beyond $\beta_{\text{ZB}}$. The interpretation acquires independent content only when $n(T)$ is obtained from the internal temperature profile of Papers \#24 and \#51. Such a derivation would determine $\beta_{\text{ZB}}$ independently of the anomalous moment and would convert the bridge equation of Appendix~\ref{app:bridge} from a relation into a derivation.

\paragraph{The microscopic origin of $n > 1$.} In water the index arises from polarizable constituents. The constituent that plays this role inside the electron is not identified here. The phase index, which governs Snell's law, and the group index, which governs the round-trip time, need not coincide; Eqs.~(\ref{eq:n}) and~(\ref{eq:thetac}) identify them, and a difference between the two would make the critical angle and the carrier frequency two independent quantities. Because the carrier is built from two discrete frequencies, the group index takes the discrete form $n_g = 2n(2\omega) - n(\omega)$ (Appendix~\ref{app:discrete}), and the identification holds when the index is the same at $\omega$ and $2\omega$.

\paragraph{A factor of two.} With $n = 1$, Eq.~(\ref{eq:nuZB}) gives $c/\lambda_C = m_ec^2/h = 1.236\times10^{20}$~Hz, one half of the Dirac value~(\ref{eq:nuD}). The factor $2/\beta_{\text{ZB}} = 49.42$ between the two predictions thus decomposes into $1/\beta_{\text{ZB}}$, attributed here to the internal index, and a factor of two that the present argument does not account for.

\section{Conclusion}

The objection that the Dirac equation employs $c$ while the 0-Sphere model assigns $0.04047\,c$ to the internal motion rests on identifying the invariant speed of the Lorentz group with the speed of an internal carrier. Once the two are separated, the 0-Sphere model retains the former and revises only the latter, in the manner of a refractive medium whose rest frame is the electron rest frame. Fizeau's experiment establishes that such a medium-internal speed is relativistically admissible, and the Foldy--Wouthuysen representation establishes that the Dirac eigenvalues $\pm c$ do not fix it. The optical metric $\mathcal{G} = n^2(T)\,g$ of Paper \#51 provides the geometric setting, in which the internal index takes the value $1/\beta_{\text{ZB}} = 24.71$.

The two theories make competing predictions for the same phenomenon: $5.00\times10^{18}$~Hz (photon energy $20.7$~keV) for the 0-Sphere model and $2.47\times10^{20}$~Hz (photon energy $1.022$~MeV) for the Dirac ZB. Quantum simulations of the ZB in trapped ions~\cite{Gerritsma2010} reproduce the frequency of the simulated Hamiltonian by construction and do not discriminate between them. A direct search in the X-ray band around $20$~keV for a resonant signature of the free electron is the experimental test.

\subsection{Two Kernels and the Photon Sphere}
\label{app:split}

\textbf{[0SM reading]} In the standard theory the electron is an elementary particle with no internal parts. The 0-Sphere model describes it by three elements: a kernel $A$, a kernel $B$, and the energy in transit between them, which the series calls the photon sphere (Fig.~\ref{fig:model}a). The name of the model comes from the zero-dimensional sphere $S^0 = \{-1,+1\}$, two points with nothing between them, which the model assigns to the two kernels. The kernels are separated by $\lambda_C/2$, where $\lambda_C = h/(m_ec) = 2.426\times10^{-12}$~m is the Compton wavelength~\cite{H51}. The existence of this structure is an assumption of the model, not an observation.

\textbf{[standard]} With an internal phase $\theta$, the fraction of the energy held at each kernel is $\cos^2(\theta/2)$ at $A$ and $\sin^2(\theta/2)$ at $B$. The identity
\begin{equation}
\cos^4\frac{\theta}{2} + \sin^4\frac{\theta}{2} + \frac12\sin^2\theta = 1
\label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
holds for every $\theta$.

\textbf{[0SM reading]} The three terms of Eq.~(\ref{eq:identity}) are read as the energy held at $A$, the energy held at $B$, and the energy in transit~\cite{H65}. At $\theta = 0$ all the energy is at $A$ and nothing moves. At $\theta = \pi/2$ each kernel holds one quarter and one half is in transit. At $\theta = 2\pi/3$ the three shares are $1/16$, $9/16$ and $6/16$. At $\theta = \pi$ all the energy is at $B$ and the transport has stopped again. The energy therefore does not jump from $A$ to $B$: the transit term rises from zero to its maximum and falls back. This gradual passage is the reason, used in Sec.~\ref{sec:two}, that each transfer is a wave group of finite duration.

% Appendix A.2: The Internal Clock
% ----------------------------------------
\subsection{The Internal Clock}
\label{app:clock}

\textbf{[0SM reading]} The model distinguishes two rates inside the electron~\cite{H65}. The phase of each Dirac branch advances at the light-speed reference
\begin{equation}
\nu_C = \frac{c}{\lambda_C} = \frac{m_ec^2}{h} = 1.2356\times10^{20}\ \text{Hz}.
\label{eq:nuC}
\end{equation}
The transport of energy from one kernel to the other runs at the speed $\beta_{\text{ZB}}c$. One cycle of Eq.~(\ref{eq:identity}) is a round trip of the photon sphere from $A$ to $B$ and back, a path of length $\lambda_C$, so that the transport frequency is $\nu_{\text{ZB}} = \beta_{\text{ZB}}c/\lambda_C = \beta_{\text{ZB}}\nu_C$, Eq.~(\ref{eq:nuZB}). The speed $\beta_{\text{ZB}}c$ is not the speed of the electron as a whole; the electron may be at rest while the energy inside it is carried between the kernels. In the language of the present Letter, $\nu_C$ is the frequency that the carrier would have at the invariant speed and $\nu_{\text{ZB}}$ the frequency that it has at the group velocity $c/n$, so that $\nu_C/\nu_{\text{ZB}} = n$.

\subsection{The Bridge Equation}
\label{app:bridge}

\textbf{[0SM reading]} The value $\beta_{\text{ZB}} = 0.040472$ is not fitted. Paper \#10~\cite{H10} identifies the anomalous magnetic moment with the fractional Lorentz contraction of the internal transport path, so that the Lorentz factor of the transport is $\gamma_L = 1 + \lvert a\rvert$. For the speed, Paper \#62~\cite{H62} uses the root-mean-square placement of the momentum-carrying term, whose mean-to-peak ratio has square root $1/\sqrt{2}$, which gives
\begin{equation}
\gamma_v = 1 + \frac{\lvert a\rvert}{\sqrt{2}}, \qquad \beta_{\text{ZB}} = \sqrt{1 - \gamma_v^{-2}} .
\label{eq:bridge}
\end{equation}
For the electron, $a_e = 1.15965218059\times10^{-3}$~\cite{Fan2023} gives $\beta_{\text{ZB}}^2 = 0.0016379809$ and $\beta_{\text{ZB}} = 0.040472$, the canonical value of the series. To first order, $\beta_{\text{ZB}} \simeq [\alpha/(\sqrt{2}\pi)]^{1/2} = 0.04053$, because $a_e \simeq \alpha/(2\pi)$. The bridge equation is a relation of the model and not of the standard theory; it is checked by measuring $\beta_{\text{ZB}}$.

\subsection{The Three-Layer Hierarchy}
\label{app:layers}

\textbf{[0SM reading]} The single number $\beta_{\text{ZB}}$ divides the energy scales of the electron into three layers by powers of $\beta_{\text{ZB}}$:
\begin{align}
\beta^0:&\quad m_ec^2 = 511\ \text{keV},
\notag\\
\beta^1:&\quad \beta_{\text{ZB}}m_ec^2 = 20.7\ \text{keV},
\notag\\
\beta^2:&\quad \tfrac12\beta_{\text{ZB}}^2m_ec^2 = 419\ \text{eV}.
\label{eq:layers}
\end{align}
The first is the rest energy, the second the energy of the internal line at $\nu_{\text{ZB}}$, and the third the floor identified in Paper \#46~\cite{H46}. The anomalous magnetic moment lives on the $\beta^2$ layer, since $a_e \simeq \gamma - 1 \simeq \beta_{\text{ZB}}^2/2$ up to the factor of Eq.~(\ref{eq:bridge}). The departure of the drag coefficient from unity, $1/n^2 = \beta_{\text{ZB}}^2$, falls on the same layer.

\subsection{Thermal Geodesics and the Optical Metric}
\label{app:optical}

\textbf{[standard]} In geometrical optics, light in a medium of refractive index $n(\bm{x})$ follows paths that make the optical length $\int n\,ds$ stationary (Fermat's principle). These paths are the geodesics of the optical metric $n^2 g$, and a region of higher index bends light toward itself and slows it.

\textbf{[0SM reading]} Paper \#24~\cite{H24} introduced thermal geodesics, the paths along which internal energy is transported, determined by the internal temperature $T$. Paper \#51~\cite{H51} showed that the thermal metric coincides with the optical metric of a medium with index $n(T)$, Eq.~(\ref{eq:optical}), and that thermal geodesics obey the corresponding Fermat principle. The present Letter does not add this structure. It assigns to the index the value $n = 1/\beta_{\text{ZB}}$ in the internal region, Eq.~(\ref{eq:nT}), and draws the consequences that do not depend on how $n(T)$ varies.

% Appendix A.6: Two Uses of the Word Radiation
% ----------------------------------------
\subsection{Two Uses of the Word Radiation}
\label{app:radiation}

\textbf{[0SM reading]} The series uses the word radiation, as in the radiation gradient of Paper \#1~\cite{H01}, for the transfer of energy from one kernel to the other inside the electron. In electrodynamics the same word means the emission of electromagnetic waves to the exterior. The two meanings are distinct and are kept apart throughout this Letter.

A free electron at rest does not emit electromagnetic waves, and nothing in this Letter implies that it does. The transfer between the kernels is internal: the energy identity~(\ref{eq:identity}) holds at every phase, the total $E_0$ is conserved, and no part of the transferred energy leaves the electron. Every statement made here about the radiation gradient, the wave group and its envelope refers to this internal transfer. Whether, and how, the internal transfer couples to external photons, through absorption or scattering, is a separate question, which this Letter does not address.

\subsection{Why a Group Velocity Belongs in This Model}
\label{app:why_belongs}

It is not obvious at first sight why the notion of a group velocity should enter a model of the electron at all. In the Dirac theory the electron has no internal parts, and the ZB is the beat between two branches of a single state; there is no medium and nothing that travels through one. The reason lies in the bookkeeping of the 0-Sphere model itself.

The identity~(\ref{eq:identity}) describes how the energy moves from kernel $A$ to kernel $B$, and Fig.~\ref{fig:group}(a) shows that it does not move all at once. The share at $A$ falls continuously, the share at $B$ rises continuously, and in between a part of the energy is on its way. The transit term starts at zero, reaches its maximum of one half when the two kernels hold equal shares, and returns to zero when the transfer is complete. Each transfer therefore has a beginning and an end, and a signal with a beginning and an end is not a single frequency but a superposition of frequencies.

For such a signal two speeds are defined. The phase velocity is the speed of an individual crest, and the group velocity is the speed of the envelope that contains the crests (Fig.~\ref{fig:group}b). The energy travels with the envelope. A familiar example is a light pulse in an optical fibre: in silica at telecommunication wavelengths the group index is about $1.47$, so that the pulse, and the information and energy it carries, advances at about $2.0\times10^{8}$~m/s, while the crests inside it move at a slightly different speed. The quantity that the 0-Sphere model fixes from the anomalous magnetic moment is the speed at which the energy is carried from one kernel to the other~\cite{H65}, which is the speed of the envelope. The group velocity is therefore not imported into the model from outside; it is the name, in the language of wave propagation, for the speed that the model already defines.

% Appendix B.2: The Question That Was Open
% ----------------------------------------
\subsection{The Question That Was Open}
\label{app:why_open}

Since Paper \#10 the series has carried the value $v_{\text{ZB}} = 0.04047\,c$, fixed with no free parameter. The value stood, however, in three uneasy positions.

\paragraph{Beside the Dirac equation.} The Dirac velocity operator has eigenvalues $\pm c$, and the standard reading of the ZB is a motion at $c$. A subluminal internal speed appeared to contradict the equation from which the series itself takes the electron's spin and the value $g = 2$.

\paragraph{Outside the geometry.} Paper \#51 established that internal energy transport follows the geodesics of an optical metric $n^2(T)\,g$. The number $0.04047$ entered nowhere in that structure. It was an output of the bridge equation, and the geometry did not say where it lived.

\paragraph{Between two clocks.} Paper \#65 separated two rates inside the electron, the light-speed reference $\nu_C = c/\lambda_C$ and the transport frequency $\nu_{\text{ZB}}$. Their ratio is $\beta_{\text{ZB}}$, but the two rates were recorded as separate entries, and the ratio had no physical reading of its own.

% ----------------------------------------
% Appendix B.3: What the Concept Opens
% ----------------------------------------
\subsection{What the Concept Opens}
\label{app:why_opens}

Reading $v_{\text{ZB}}$ as the group velocity of a carrier in an internal medium of index $n = 1/\beta_{\text{ZB}}$ changes each of the three positions, and adds two consequences.

\paragraph{The conflict becomes a distinction.} The $c$ of the Dirac equation is the invariant speed; the $0.04047\,c$ of the model is a group velocity inside a medium. The two belong to different categories, as do the $c$ in the Lorentz transformation and the $c/1.33$ of light in water. The model keeps Lorentz covariance, spin and $g = 2$ unchanged (Secs.~\ref{sec:two}--\ref{sec:fw}).

\paragraph{The number acquires a place.} In the optical metric of Paper \#51, $\beta_{\text{ZB}}$ is the reciprocal of the value that the index takes inside the electron, Eq.~(\ref{eq:nT}). The question why the internal speed is about $0.04\,c$ becomes the question why the internal index is about $25$, which is a question about the internal temperature profile $n(T)$ and can be attacked within the geometry of the series.

\paragraph{The two clocks become one quantity.} The light-speed reference is the frequency that a carrier would have at the invariant speed, and the transport frequency is the frequency it has at the group velocity. Their ratio $\nu_C/\nu_{\text{ZB}}$ is the index $n$ (Appendix~\ref{app:clock}).

\paragraph{A route from relation to derivation.} At present $\beta_{\text{ZB}}$ follows from the measured anomalous moment through the bridge equation, which is a relation of the model and not a derivation. If $n(T)$ is obtained from the internal temperature profile, $\beta_{\text{ZB}}$ is fixed independently of the anomalous moment, and the bridge equation becomes a prediction that the measured moment can confirm or refute (open item O1 of Appendix~\ref{app:interface}).

\paragraph{A contrast that experiment can decide.} With the conflict removed, the difference between the two theories is no longer a matter of consistency but of measurement: $5.00\times10^{18}$~Hz ($20.7$~keV) against $2.47\times10^{20}$~Hz ($1.022$~MeV). The prediction of the 0-Sphere model lies in the X-ray band, where it can be searched for.

Figure~\ref{fig:flow} summarizes these five changes.

\textbf{[standard]} The identity~(\ref{eq:identity}) fixes not only how much energy is at each kernel but also how fast it moves. Writing $T_A = E_0\cos^4(\theta/2)$, $T_B = E_0\sin^4(\theta/2)$ and $K = \tfrac12E_0\sin^2\theta$, the rates per unit phase are
\begin{align}
\frac{1}{E_0}\frac{dT_A}{d\theta} &= -2\cos^3\frac{\theta}{2}\sin\frac{\theta}{2},
\notag\\
\frac{1}{E_0}\frac{dT_B}{d\theta} &= 2\sin^3\frac{\theta}{2}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}.
\label{eq:rates}
\end{align}
Their difference and their sum are
\begin{align}
\frac{1}{E_0}\left(\frac{dT_B}{d\theta} - \frac{dT_A}{d\theta}\right) &= \sin\theta,
\label{eq:gradient}\\
\frac{dT_A}{d\theta} + \frac{dT_B}{d\theta} + \frac{dK}{d\theta} &= 0.
\label{eq:conservation}
\end{align}
The second line is the conservation of energy. The first is the radiation gradient between the two kernels, which Paper \#1~\cite{H01} introduced in the form of a sine wave; here it appears as the difference of the derivatives of the two kernel energies.

\textbf{[0SM reading]} Equation~(\ref{eq:gradient}) excludes a picture in which kernel $A$ radiates toward kernel $B$ with a constant amplitude for the duration of the transfer, as a lamp radiates once it is switched on; radiation is meant here in the internal sense of Appendix~\ref{app:radiation}. The gradient is zero at the start of the transfer, rises to its maximum when the two kernels hold equal shares, and returns to zero when the transfer is complete (Fig.~\ref{fig:envelope}a). The amplitude of the transfer is a function of the phase, and the function is fixed by the identity. This point carries the weight of the group-velocity reading. A transfer of constant amplitude would be a single steady stream, for which only a speed of flow is defined; a transfer whose amplitude rises and falls within each cycle is a wave group, and its envelope moves at the group velocity.

The same conclusion follows from the energies themselves. Expanding Eq.~(\ref{eq:identity}) term by term,
\begin{align}
\frac{T_A}{E_0} &= \frac38 + \frac12\cos\theta + \frac18\cos2\theta,
\notag\\
\frac{T_B}{E_0} &= \frac38 - \frac12\cos\theta + \frac18\cos2\theta,
\notag\\
\frac{K}{E_0} &= \frac14 - \frac14\cos2\theta.
\label{eq:fourier}
\end{align}
The energy held at a kernel is a superposition of the fundamental and the second harmonic with weights $1/2$ and $1/8$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:envelope}b). A superposition of two frequencies is the smallest wave group, and its weights are not adjustable: they are the coefficients of the quartic energy of Paper \#65~\cite{H65}. The gradient~(\ref{eq:gradient}) is by itself a single sinusoid in $\theta$; the wave-group character resides in the energies held at the kernels, Eq.~(\ref{eq:fourier}), and in the fact that each transfer runs in one direction between two zeros of the gradient.

\paragraph{Comparison with a Gaussian wave packet.} In quantum mechanics a localized particle is commonly represented by a plane wave multiplied by a Gaussian envelope. The envelope acts as a window, and its width is chosen as an initial condition. Under the free Schr\"odinger equation the window widens with time, because the frequency depends quadratically on the wave number: a packet of initial width $\sigma_0$ has width $\sigma(t) = \sigma_0[1 + (\hbar t/2m\sigma_0^2)^2]^{1/2}$. The envelope of the 0-Sphere transfer is of a different kind. It is not an initial condition but a consequence of the identity, and the identity holds at every cycle, so that the same envelope is restored at each transfer. The model therefore contains no mechanism by which the transfer would widen from one cycle to the next. A Gaussian window may serve as a convenient approximation in future numerical work, but the envelope it approximates is fixed in advance, and in the reading of the model it is this recurrence of one and the same envelope, cycle after cycle, that keeps the electron a localized object over time.

% Appendix B.5: Continuous in Phase, Discrete in Frequency
% ----------------------------------------
\subsection{Continuous in Phase, Discrete in Frequency}
\label{app:discrete}

The radiation gradient~(\ref{eq:gradient}) is a continuous function of the phase: it rises and falls smoothly, with no jumps and no gaps. When the same transfer is described as a wave group, however, a discrete structure appears. Only a few definite frequencies occur, and no frequency between them. This subsection derives that structure step by step from the elementary trigonometric identities, because the derivation shows where the discreteness comes from.

\paragraph{Step 1: the shares.} The half-angle formulas
\begin{equation}
\cos^2\frac{\theta}{2} = \frac{1 + \cos\theta}{2}, \qquad \sin^2\frac{\theta}{2} = \frac{1 - \cos\theta}{2}
\label{eq:halfangle}
\end{equation}
give the shares of the energy held at $A$ and at $B$. Each share is a constant plus a single cosine of frequency $\omega$, where $\omega$ denotes the rate at which $\theta$ advances. A share alone contains one oscillating frequency.

\paragraph{Step 2: the energies.} In the 0-Sphere model the energy held at a kernel is the square of its share~\cite{H65}. Squaring Eq.~(\ref{eq:halfangle}),
\begin{equation}
\cos^4\frac{\theta}{2} = \frac{1 + 2\cos\theta + \cos^2\theta}{4}.
\label{eq:square}
\end{equation}
The square of a cosine of frequency $\omega$ contains frequency $2\omega$, by the double-angle formula
\begin{equation}
\cos^2\theta = \frac{1 + \cos2\theta}{2}.
\label{eq:doubleangle}
\end{equation}
Substituting Eq.~(\ref{eq:doubleangle}) into Eq.~(\ref{eq:square}) gives
\begin{equation}
\cos^4\frac{\theta}{2} = \frac38 + \frac12\cos\theta + \frac18\cos2\theta,
\label{eq:TAfourier}
\end{equation}
and in the same way $\sin^4(\theta/2) = \tfrac38 - \tfrac12\cos\theta + \tfrac18\cos2\theta$. The transit term follows from Eq.~(\ref{eq:doubleangle}) with sine in place of cosine: $\tfrac12\sin^2\theta = \tfrac14 - \tfrac14\cos2\theta$. These are the expansions~(\ref{eq:fourier}).

\paragraph{Step 3: the identity, frequency by frequency.} Adding the three expansions, each frequency balances on its own (Table~\ref{tab:lines}). The constant parts add to $\tfrac38 + \tfrac38 + \tfrac14 = 1$. The parts at $\omega$ cancel between $A$ and $B$, $+\tfrac12 - \tfrac12 = 0$. The parts at $2\omega$ cancel between the two kernels and the photon sphere, $\tfrac18 + \tfrac18 - \tfrac14 = 0$. The identity~(\ref{eq:identity}) is therefore not one balance but three, one for each frequency, and the frequency $\omega$ and the frequency $2\omega$ each describe a separate exchange. The fundamental is the exchange between $A$ and $B$, antisymmetric in the two kernels; it is the only part that moves the energy from one side to the other. The second harmonic is the exchange between the kernels taken together and the photon sphere, symmetric in the two kernels; it moves energy into transit and back without moving it from one side to the other.

\paragraph{Step 4: why the spectrum stops at $2\omega$.} A periodic function generally contains infinitely many harmonics; a train of rectangular pulses, for example, contains every odd multiple of its fundamental. The expansions~(\ref{eq:fourier}) stop at $2\omega$, and the reason is the power of the energy. A share is a polynomial of degree one in $\cos\theta$ and contains frequencies up to $\omega$. An energy, the square of a share, is a polynomial of degree two in $\cos\theta$ and contains frequencies up to $2\omega$, and no further. Had the model taken the energy proportional to the share itself, the energy at each kernel would have contained a single oscillating frequency, and no wave group would have formed. The step from share to energy in Paper \#65, the squaring that gives the quartic energy, is what adds the second frequency. The number of lines is fixed by the power of the energy, and their weights by the identity.

\paragraph{Step 5: the group velocity of two lines.} For a continuous band of frequencies the group velocity is the derivative $d\omega/dk$. For two discrete lines it becomes a difference quotient. The sum-to-product identity
\begin{equation}
\cos a + \cos b = 2\cos\frac{a - b}{2}\cos\frac{a + b}{2}
\label{eq:sumtoproduct}
\end{equation}
shows this directly. With $a = \omega t - k_1x$ and $b = 2\omega t - k_2x$, the first factor, the envelope, depends on $\omega t - (k_2 - k_1)x$ and moves at
\begin{equation}
v_g = \frac{2\omega - \omega}{k_2 - k_1} = \frac{\Delta\omega}{\Delta k}.
\label{eq:vgdiscrete}
\end{equation}
Unequal amplitudes, such as the weights $1/2$ and $1/8$, change the depth of the modulation but not the speed of the envelope. In a medium with index $n_1$ at frequency $\omega$ and $n_2$ at frequency $2\omega$, the wave numbers are $k_1 = n_1\omega/c$ and $k_2 = 2n_2\omega/c$, so that
\begin{equation}
v_g = \frac{c}{2n_2 - n_1}, \qquad n_g = 2n(2\omega) - n(\omega).
\label{eq:ngdiscrete}
\end{equation}
The group index of the internal carrier is thus a discrete quantity, determined by the index at two frequencies only. When the index is the same at both, $n_g = n$ and the group velocity equals $c/n$, which is the identification made in Eq.~(\ref{eq:n}). Whether $n(2\omega)$ equals $n(\omega)$ is the precise form of open item O2.

\paragraph{What the discreteness adds.} The transfer is continuous when it is followed through the phase and discrete when it is resolved into frequencies. Both descriptions are exact, and they are two readings of the same identity. The second reading carries information that the first does not show at a glance: the identity holds separately at each frequency; the exchange between the two kernels and the exchange between the kernels and the photon sphere are distinct and occur at distinct frequencies; the number of frequencies is set by the power of the energy; and the group velocity of the carrier is fixed by the index at two frequencies only. In the reading of the model, the internal transfer of the electron is continuous in its course and discrete in its composition.

\section{Inputs, Results and Open Items}

This appendix restates the content of the Letter as a list, so that later papers of the series can cite each item by its label.

\item[I2.] The thermal metric is the optical metric $\mathcal{G} = n^2(T)\,g$ (Paper \#51; Appendix~\ref{app:optical}).

\paragraph{Results.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.4em]
\item[R1.] The speed $c$ of the Dirac equation is the invariant speed of the Lorentz group; the 0-Sphere model retains it without modification (Sec.~\ref{sec:two}).
\item[R2.] The speed $0.04047\,c$ is the group velocity of the internal carrier, $v_{\text{ZB}} = c/n$ with $n = 1/\beta_{\text{ZB}} = 24.71$, Eq.~(\ref{eq:n}).
\item[R3.] A medium-internal speed $c/n$ is admissible in special relativity; the Fresnel drag coefficient of the electron is $1 - \beta_{\text{ZB}}^2$, Eq.~(\ref{eq:drag}).
\item[R4.] The Dirac eigenvalues $\pm c$ belong to one choice of position operator and do not fix the carrier speed, Eq.~(\ref{eq:FW}).
\item[R5.] Within the optical metric of I2, the internal index is $n(T_{\text{int}}) = 1/\beta_{\text{ZB}}$, Eq.~(\ref{eq:nT}).
\item[R6.] At any boundary where the index returns to unity, $\sin\theta_c = \beta_{\text{ZB}}$, $\theta_c = 2.32^\circ$, independently of the shape of the boundary, Eq.~(\ref{eq:thetac}).
\item[R7.] Competing predictions: $\nu_{\text{ZB}} = 5.00\times10^{18}$~Hz (0-Sphere) against $\nu_{\text{D}} = 2.47\times10^{20}$~Hz (Dirac).
\item[R8.] The amplitude of the transfer is not constant: the radiation gradient is $\sin\theta$, the difference of the derivatives of the two kernel energies, and the kernel energies are superpositions of the fundamental and the second harmonic with fixed weights, Eqs.~(\ref{eq:gradient}) and~(\ref{eq:fourier}). The envelope is restored at every cycle and does not widen.
\item[R9.] The transfer is continuous in the phase and discrete in frequency: the kernel energies contain exactly the frequencies $0$, $\omega$ and $2\omega$; the identity balances separately at each frequency; and the group index is the difference quotient $n_g = 2n(2\omega) - n(\omega)$, Eqs.~(\ref{eq:vgdiscrete}) and~(\ref{eq:ngdiscrete}).
\end{itemize}

\item[O5.] The coupling of the internal carrier to gravitation.
\item[O6.] The coupling of the internal transfer to external photons, through absorption or scattering. The word radiation in this Letter denotes the internal transfer only (Appendix~\ref{app:radiation}).
