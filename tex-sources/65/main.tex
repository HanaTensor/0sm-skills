\title{From Zero and One to the Electron: Path-Connectedness, the Double Cover,\\ and the Geometric Origin of the Quartic Energy in the 0-Sphere Model}

\author{Satoshi Hanamura}
\email{hana.tensor@gmail.com}

\date{October 10, 2026}

\begin{abstract}
This paper traces where the integers of a model of the electron come from. The model is the 0-Sphere model, in which the electron is two separated kernels exchanging a captured photon.
We start from arithmetic: the integers are fixed by $0$ and $1$, the numbers that do not change when squared are exactly $\{0,1\}$, and the invertible integers are $\{\pm1\}$, which is the zero-sphere $S^0$.
The model's two energy identities share the right-hand side $1$, yet the bosonic identity $n_A+n_B=1$ is solved by a whole segment $D^1$, whereas the kernel part of the fermionic identity $(n_A+n_B)^2=1$ is solved only by the two endpoints, $S^0=\partial D^1$; the cross term is exactly the seat that lets energy travel between the two separated points.
Path-connectedness therefore enters at the foundations, and the next topological step, a closed path that is not simply connected, yields an integer winding number that the model identifies with electric charge; a point vortex in fluid mechanics gives the picture, and quantized circulation in superfluids shows that the integer and its size come from different sources.
We retain and sharpen the earlier results of this paper: the amplitude behind the identity obeys a first-order spinor equation unitarily equivalent to the Dirac rest solutions, and the energy exchange between the two kernels is exactly the interference term between the positive- and negative-energy branches, so that zitterbewegung is read as a real internal oscillation and the negative branch as the absorbing half of a paired emission and absorption rather than as an antiparticle; the complex unit is the symplectic structure of the internal oscillator, and the quartic energies are quadratic forms on the Bloch sphere pulled back through the Hopf double cover.
We then define the internal time as the transport cycle between the kernels, fixed by the anomalous magnetic moment through $\beta=0.04047$, show that it runs exactly $\beta$ times slower than the Dirac branch phase, and show that the kernel separation is the transport speed times one tick, $\lambda_C=\beta c\,T_{\mathrm{tr}}$.
Metric structure is not needed while the two kernels merely sit; it enters with transport, together with the constitutive relation and the fine-structure constant.
We state explicitly that the model derives the integer of charge, its existence, quantization, conservation and sign, but not its size: the fine-structure constant is not derived here, nor is the coupling to the electromagnetic potential or the light-cone structure.
The decisive test is experimental: the internal transport speed $\beta c\approx0.04\,c$, fixed with no free parameter, is unmeasured and would confirm or exclude the internal clock.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Integers in physics and in the model}

Physics is full of integers. Charges come in whole multiples, angular momentum comes in half-integer steps~\cite{ref:uhlenbeck,ref:sakurai}, and the number of states of a spin-$\tfrac12$ particle is two. In the standard theory these integers are mostly put in by hand: one chooses a symmetry group, and the integers appear as labels of its representations. A model that claims to describe the inside of the electron owes the reader a better answer. It should say where its integers come from, which of them it can derive, and which of them it cannot.

This paper takes up that question for the 0-Sphere model (abbreviated 0SM below). Each section states the problem it addresses and why the step is needed, and then gives numbers, a worked example, and a short summary; the paper can be read without the earlier papers of the series. The flow of the whole argument is collected in Fig.~\ref{fig:flow} at the end.

\subsection{The model in one paragraph}

The 0-Sphere model represents the electron not as a point but as a small internal system: two perfect black-body \emph{kernels}, $A$ and $B$, separated by about one Compton wavelength, together with a captured photon, the \emph{photon sphere}, that carries energy back and forth between them~\cite{ref:p1}. The name comes from topology: the zero-dimensional sphere $S^0$ is a pair of points, and the two kernels are such a pair. The bookkeeping of the internal energy is carried by one identity, cited by every paper of the series,
\begin{equation}
1 = \cos^4\!\tfrac{\theta}{2} + \sin^4\!\tfrac{\theta}{2} + \tfrac12\sin^2\theta ,
\label{eq:central}
\end{equation}
multiplied by the total energy $E_0$. The first two terms are the thermal potential energies of kernels $A$ and $B$, and the third is the kinetic energy of the photon sphere in transit. The angle $\theta$ advances with time; what clock measures that advance is the subject of Sec.~\ref{sec:time}.

\subsection{Four results of this paper}

Equation~\eqref{eq:central} is an algebraic identity, true for every $\theta$. Its physical content therefore lies not in the fact that it sums to one, but in the structure it hides. We extract four pieces of that structure.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=0.25em]
\item \emph{The fourth powers.} Relativistic quantum mechanics expects energies quadratic in the amplitude~\cite{ref:dirac28}. Section~\ref{sec:quartic} shows that the fourth powers are quadratic energies seen through the spinor double cover.
\item \emph{Boson and fermion.} The photon obeys $\cos^2+\sin^2=1$, the electron the identity above, and both right-hand sides are $1$. Section~\ref{sec:twopictures} shows that the difference is the shape of the solution set: a segment versus its two endpoints.
\item \emph{The origin of integers.} Sections~\ref{sec:arith}, \ref{sec:path} and~\ref{sec:winding} trace them from the arithmetic of $0$ and $1$ through path-connectedness to winding numbers, which the model identifies with charge.
\item \emph{Internal time and distance.} Section~\ref{sec:time} defines the internal time as the transport cycle, fixed by the anomalous magnetic moment, and builds the kernel separation from it.
\end{enumerate}
Section~\ref{sec:metric} looks ahead to the comparison of two electrons' clocks and to where the metric enters. Section~\ref{sec:exp} lists what experiment can decide, and Sec.~\ref{sec:limits} states the distance from standard theory without softening it. Appendix~\ref{app:charge} derives the topological properties of charge in full.

\subsection{Conventions: three layers and two kinds of statement}
\label{sec:conv}

Two habits will prevent most misreadings.

\paragraph{Three layers.} The two kernels are described at three levels, and we never mix them. The \emph{amplitude} layer carries $a=\cos(\theta/2)$ and $b=\sin(\theta/2)$, which can be negative. The \emph{occupation} layer carries $n_A=a^2$ and $n_B=b^2$, which are non-negative and sum to one; the kernel \emph{temperature} lives here. The \emph{energy} layer carries $T_A=E_0 n_A^2$ and $T_B=E_0 n_B^2$. The fourth power in Eq.~\eqref{eq:central} is therefore a fourth power of the amplitude, but only a square of the occupation.

\paragraph{Two kinds of statement.} Following the structural overview of the series~\cite{ref:p68}, we mark statements that belong to textbook physics or mathematics as \textbf{[standard]} and statements that belong to the interpretation of the 0-Sphere model as \textbf{[0SM reading]} wherever confusion could arise. A reader may accept every \textbf{[standard]} statement and still reject the model; the paper is written so that this division is always visible.

\section{Zero and One: The Arithmetic Seed}
\label{sec:arith}

\paragraph{Motivation.} The paper begins with the arithmetic of $0$ and $1$ because the three facts below are the patterns that the rest of the paper finds again inside the electron. Seeing them first in their simplest setting makes the later sections recognisable rather than surprising.

\subsection{The integers are fixed by 0 and 1}

\textbf{[standard]} As an additive group, the integers $\mathbb{Z}$ are generated by $1$ alone: every integer is $1+1+\cdots$ or its negative, and $0$ is the empty sum. As a ring, $\mathbb{Z}$ is fixed by $0$ and $1$ together~\cite{ref:lang}: for any ring $R$ there is exactly one structure-preserving map $\mathbb{Z}\to R$, the one sending $0\mapsto0$ and $1\mapsto1$. In this precise sense, once $0$ and $1$ are given, the whole of $\mathbb{Z}$ is given. This is prior to any notation for numbers: a positional system with base $b$ needs the digits $0,\dots,b-1$, so the decimal system needs ten digits, and only base two gets by with $0$ and $1$. The structure, not the notation, is what we use.

\subsection{The numbers that do not change when squared}

\textbf{[standard]} A number is \emph{idempotent} if squaring it changes nothing, $n^2=n$. Among the real numbers the solutions are exactly
\begin{equation}
n(n-1)=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n\in\{0,1\} = \mathbb{Z}\cap[0,1].
\label{eq:idem}
\end{equation}
Between $0$ and $1$ every number shrinks when squared; only the two ends survive. Section~\ref{sec:twopictures} meets this equation again, as the condition under which the electron's kernel energies alone add up to the total.

\subsection{The invertible integers form the zero-sphere}

\textbf{[standard]} Only two integers have integer inverses, $+1$ and $-1$. They form the group of units
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{Z}^{\times}=\{+1,-1\} = S^0 ,
\label{eq:units}
\end{equation}
the zero-dimensional sphere: the set of points at unit distance from the origin of the real line. \textbf{[0SM reading]} The same two-element set appears twice inside the electron: as the sign flip $\chi\to-\chi$ of the amplitude after one full turn (Sec.~\ref{sec:quartic}), and as the two signs of charge, $e^{\mp}$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:winding}).

\section{One Normalization, Two Pictures}
\label{sec:twopictures}

\paragraph{Motivation.} The photon's identity and the electron's identity both equal one. The difference between the two particles therefore lies not in the total but in \emph{who carries it}.

\subsection{The same one, read at two degrees}

Write the occupations of the two kernels as $n_A=\cos^2(\theta/2)$ and $n_B=\sin^2(\theta/2)$. The bosonic identity of the model~\cite{ref:p38} and the fermionic identity~\eqref{eq:central} are then
\begin{align}
\text{boson:}\quad & n_A+n_B=1, \label{eq:boson}\\
\text{fermion:}\quad & (n_A+n_B)^2 = n_A^2+n_B^2+2n_An_B = 1. \label{eq:fermion}
\end{align}
The second is the square of the first, and $2n_An_B=\tfrac12\sin^2\theta$ is the cross term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:central}. The same normalization is read at degree one and at degree two~\cite{ref:p38}. Expanded, the fermionic identity has three terms: one seat for kernel $A$, one for the photon sphere in transit, and one for kernel $B$.

\subsection{A worked example: one full turn, step by step}

\paragraph{Why this is needed.} Equation~\eqref{eq:central} holds for every $\theta$, and a reader may suspect that an identity true for all values says nothing. Following one turn in steps of $\pi/2$ shows what the identity \emph{records}: which seat holds the energy at each moment, and how the amplitude changes sign while every observable returns. Table~\ref{tab:trace} gives the numbers.

\subsection{Two solution sets: a segment and its two ends}

We can now state the difference between the two particles in the language of shapes.

\begin{proposition}[Two solution sets]
\label{prop:sets}
On the occupation segment $\{(n_A,n_B):n_A,n_B\ge0,\ n_A+n_B=1\}$, the bosonic identity holds on the whole segment $D^1=[0,1]$, which is path-connected and one-dimensional. For every degree $p>1$, the kernel part $n_A^p+n_B^p=1$ holds only at the two endpoints $\{0,1\}=S^0=\partial D^1$, which are not path-connected.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $p>1$ the function $f(x)=x^p+(1-x)^p$ is strictly convex on $[0,1]$ with $f(0)=f(1)=1$, so $f<1$ in the interior. For $p=1$, $f\equiv1$.
\end{proof}

\textbf{[0SM reading]} The boson is the segment; the fermion's kernels are its boundary. \emph{Zero is the boundary of one}: $S^0=\partial D^1$. The cross term $2n_An_B$ is what fills the segment back in, so that the fermionic total is again one at every point. It is the seat that energy occupies while travelling between two points that are otherwise disconnected. The same two points appear in two further places in the corpus: as the two fold points when the amplitude circle is folded onto the occupation segment, which are the critical points of the internal Morse function~\cite{ref:milnor}, and as the two branch points $\pm mc^2$ of the square root in the Dirac energy, where they appear as the edges of the Joukowski plate of the trunk analysis~\cite{ref:trunks}.

\subsection{Choice of degree: two independent criteria}

Proposition~\ref{prop:sets} separates $p=1$ from every $p>1$; it does not by itself pick $p=2$. Two independent criteria do, and they meet at the same answer.

\paragraph{Harmonicity.} The difference of kernel energies $D_p=n_A^p-n_B^p$ should be a pure harmonic oscillation, $D_p\propto\cos\theta$, so that the internal motion is simple-harmonic as assumed since Ref.~\cite{ref:p10}. Writing $u=\cos\theta$, one has $D_p=[(1+u)^p-(1-u)^p]/2^p$, and the cubic term vanishes only if $\binom{p}{3}=p(p-1)(p-2)/6=0$. The real solutions are $\{0,1,2\}$, and $p=0$ is degenerate. Harmonicity selects $\{1,2\}$ from the whole real line, without assuming that $p$ is an integer.

\paragraph{A worked check of harmonicity.} For $p=2$ the difference factorizes, $D_2=n_A^2-n_B^2=(n_A-n_B)(n_A+n_B)=\cos\theta\cdot1$, a pure harmonic. For $p=3$ it does not: $D_3=n_A^3-n_B^3=(n_A-n_B)(n_A^2+n_An_B+n_B^2)=\cos\theta\,(1-\tfrac14\sin^2\theta)$, which contains $\cos^3\theta$ and is no longer simple-harmonic. For $p=1$, $D_1=\cos\theta$ trivially. The factor $(n_A+n_B)^{p-1}$ that would be needed to make $D_p$ harmonic is available only at $p=1$ and $p=2$.

\paragraph{Counting seats.} The expansion of $(n_A+n_B)^p$ has $p+1$ terms. Two kernels and one carrier in transit need three seats, hence $p=2$. A photon has no kernels and is itself the carrier, so it needs only the two seats of $p=1$. Counting seats picks one member of the pair that harmonicity allowed.

\subsection{What does \emph{not} separate them}

A caution is needed, because an earlier draft of this paper stated otherwise. One might expect the spinor double cover to be what separates fermion from boson. It is not. The sign flip $a\to-a$, $b\to-b$ under $\theta\to\theta+2\pi$ follows from the definition $a=\cos(\theta/2)$, $b=\sin(\theta/2)$ alone, and holds for every degree $p$; the number of critical points of the internal oscillation is also independent of $p$. The double cover is shared ground, not the dividing wall. What divides the two pictures is Proposition~\ref{prop:sets} together with the seat count: whether the energy can be split into kernel seats globally, at every point of the cycle. We also do not claim that this division is the same as the spin--statistics division of standard theory, whose origin is the topology of exchanging identical particles in three dimensions~\cite{ref:pauli40,ref:fr68,ref:lm77}; the 0-Sphere model reaches the same two-way choice at a different place and for a different reason.

\established{The boson's identity is solved by the whole occupation segment $D^1$; the kernel part of the fermion's identity only by its two endpoints $S^0=\partial D^1$. The cross term is the seat of energy in transit. Harmonicity selects degrees $1$ and $2$, the seat count selects $2$ for a two-kernel particle, and the double cover is shared by both pictures rather than separating them.}

\section{Path-Connectedness: Joining Two Separated Points}
\label{sec:path}

\paragraph{Motivation.} The fermion's kernels are two disconnected points, and a disconnected set has no paths; energy nevertheless travels between them. The resolution is that the two points sit inside a larger connected region, and the cross term is the bookkeeping of the journey through it.

\subsection{From two points to a connected domain}

\textbf{[standard]} A space is \emph{path-connected} if any two of its points can be joined by a continuous path inside it. The zero-sphere $S^0$ is the simplest space that is not: its two points have nothing between them. \textbf{[0SM reading]} Reference~\cite{ref:p33} resolves this by embedding: the two kernels are discrete locations in a path-connected compact domain $\mathcal{D}$, so that discrete localisation and continuous transport coexist. Reference~\cite{ref:p51} turns this into a chain of statements: $S^0$ is not path-connected; it embeds in a sphere; the Bonnet--Myers bound limits the diameter~\cite{ref:myers}; the domain is path-connected and the two kernels are not antipodal; there is a unique thermal geodesic joining them; and the two-point phase functional along that geodesic is well defined. Proposition~\ref{prop:sets} adds the energy-side counterpart: the transit seat $2n_An_B$ is exactly what the energy budget needs for that journey.

\subsection{Gradients, curls, and the first layer of the Helmholtz decomposition}

\paragraph{Why this is needed.} The energy that drives the photon sphere is a difference of two scalar quantities, the kernel energies. A vector field that is the gradient of a scalar has no curl. A reader who knows electromagnetism will immediately ask where curl, and with it the magnetic field, could ever come from. Fluid mechanics answers this cleanly.

\textbf{[standard]} The Helmholtz decomposition writes a velocity field as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{u} = \nabla\phi + \nabla\times\bm{\psi} + \mathbf{h},
\label{eq:helmholtz}
\end{equation}
a gradient part, a rotational part, and a harmonic part~\cite{ref:helmholtz,ref:bhatia}. The harmonic part $\mathbf{h}$ has no curl and no divergence, yet is not a gradient; it exists only when the domain has holes. In differential-form language this is the Hodge decomposition~\cite{ref:hodge}, and the harmonic part counts the holes.

The drive is the gradient of the energy difference $D=(T_A-T_B)/E_0=\cos\theta$, and the kinetic energy of the photon sphere is $\tfrac12(dD/d\theta)^2=\tfrac12\sin^2\theta$, the cross term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:central}. Second, the other two parts of Eq.~\eqref{eq:helmholtz} do appear, but at different layers: the harmonic part in Sec.~\ref{sec:winding}, the rotational part in Sec.~\ref{sec:metric}.

The kernels are discrete locations inside a connected domain (Refs.~\cite{ref:p33,ref:p51}); the photon sphere travels a continuous path, the thermal geodesic. What is discrete is where energy can \emph{rest}; what is continuous is the path along which it \emph{moves}. The energy budget records both: the kernel seats for resting, the transit seat for moving.}

\established{Path-connectedness is the property the two kernels lack and the transport supplies. The drive of the photon sphere is the gradient of the energy difference, the first layer of the Helmholtz decomposition; at this layer nothing circulates.}

\section{Closing the Path: Winding Numbers and Charge}
\label{sec:winding}

\paragraph{Motivation.} So far there is a segment and its two endpoints. An integer that is conserved and carries a sign, as charge does, cannot live on a segment: a segment has no holes, so nothing can wind around anything. An integer appears when the path closes into a loop around a point it cannot cross.

\subsection{From the segment to the circle}

\textbf{[standard]} Two elementary operations relate the segment and the circle. Folding a circle onto a diameter gives a segment, with the two fold points forming $S^0$; conversely, gluing the two endpoints of a segment gives a circle, $D^1/S^0\cong S^1$. The circle is path-connected but not simply connected: a loop that goes once around cannot be shrunk to a point, and the number of times a loop goes around is an integer, $\pi_1(S^1)\cong\mathbb{Z}$~\cite{ref:hatcher,ref:nakahara}.

\textbf{[0SM reading]} The occupation segment is the shadow of the amplitude circle $z=a+ib$, folded at the two kernel points. The integer lives on the circle, not on its shadow. The circle encloses the origin of the amplitude plane only if the two kernels never empty simultaneously, and it closes on itself only if the two kernels are \emph{locked} to the same frequency, which is the normalization $|z|=1$~\cite{ref:p1,ref:p20}. Both conditions together give an integer that cannot change continuously: the winding number of $z$ around the origin. That a quarter-period stagger between the two kernels turns their scalar oscillation into a rotation was shown in Ref.~\cite{ref:p50}.

\paragraph{A worked example: why lock and normalization go together.} Keep the two kernels at the same frequency but let the second lag by a phase $\delta$: $a=\cos t$, $b=\sin(t+\delta)$. Figure~\ref{fig:delta} draws the curve for four values of $\delta$. A short calculation (Appendix~\ref{app:charge}) gives the closest approach to the origin in closed form,
\begin{equation}
\min_t|z|=\sqrt{1-|\sin\delta|},
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
which is $1$, $0.707$, $0.366$ and $0$ for $\delta=0,\pi/6,\pi/3,\pi/2$. The distance from the origin is the \emph{gap} that protects the winding number: as long as it is positive, no small disturbance can change the integer. Only at $\delta=0$ and $\delta=\pi$ is the gap the full radius at every instant, and only there is the normalization $n_A+n_B=1$ satisfied at every instant. At $\delta=\pi/2$ the gap closes, the curve passes through the origin, and the integer is lost.

\subsection{A worked example from fluid mechanics: the point vortex}

\textbf{[standard]} A point vortex in a plane~\cite{ref:batchelor,ref:saffman} has the velocity field $u_\theta=\Gamma/(2\pi r)$. Its vorticity vanishes at every point except the origin, yet the circulation around any loop enclosing the origin is $\oint\mathbf{u}\cdot d\mathbf{l}=\Gamma\neq0$. The velocity potential $\phi=\Gamma\varphi/(2\pi)$, with $\varphi$ the polar angle, exists locally but changes by $\Gamma$ on each turn, so it is not a single-valued gradient. This is the harmonic part $\mathbf{h}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:helmholtz} in its simplest form. Kelvin's theorem says that the circulation is conserved by ideal flow~\cite{ref:kelvin}. Curl-free, not a gradient, conserved, and carrying a sign set by the sense of rotation: these are the four properties we want for charge.

\subsection{Charge as a protected winding number}

\textbf{[0SM reading]} Following the trunk analysis of the corpus (trunk T2, Sec.~T2.4--T2.5)~\cite{ref:trunks}, the winding number of the locked two-kernel amplitude is identified with electric charge. Its content can be stated in one quantity, the Wronskian of the two amplitudes,
\begin{equation}
Q=\frac{2}{\omega}\big(a\,\dot b-b\,\dot a\big),
\label{eq:wronskian}
\end{equation}
which equals $+1$ for the rotor $a=\cos(\omega t/2)$, $b=\sin(\omega t/2)$ and $-1$ for the reversed sense (Fig.~\ref{fig:winding}a,b). Appendix~\ref{app:charge} derives each statement below. Each property of charge then has a standard mathematical source, as Table~\ref{tab:charge} shows. For a detuned pair, with the two kernels at different frequencies, the closed form of the winding number is the mod-$4$ Dirichlet character~\cite{ref:apostol}, $W=\chi_4(q)$ for odd frequency ratios $p:q$, which takes only the values $0,\pm1$, that is, the values $\{0\}\cup S^0$; this closed form has been checked against direct counting for all coprime odd pairs up to $301$. A detuned pair has no protected integer and is neutral, which the model reads as the neutrino. Earlier papers of the series attributed charge to a single term of the identity~\cite{ref:p42}, discussed its localization~\cite{ref:p44}, and asked whether the symmetry of the kernel pair is $SO(2)$ or $O(2)$~\cite{ref:p48}. The winding-number reading corrects the first, since a single term oscillates and is not conserved, and answers the last: the reflection $b\to-b$ that reverses the winding lies outside $SO(2)$ and is charge conjugation.

\subsection{The integer and its size come from different places}

\paragraph{Why this matters.} Table~\ref{tab:charge} ends with two rows marked \emph{not derived}. A reader may take this as a defect. Quantized circulation in condensed matter shows that it is the normal structure of any quantized winding.

\textbf{[standard]} In superfluid helium-4 the circulation around a vortex is $\Gamma=n\kappa$~\cite{ref:onsager,ref:feynman55,ref:vinen}: the integer $n$ is the winding of the condensate phase, while the size of one quantum, $\kappa=h/m_{\mathrm{He}}=9.97\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}$, comes from Planck's constant and the atomic mass. In a superconducting ring the magnetic flux is $\Phi=n\,h/(2e)$~\cite{ref:deaver,ref:doll} with $h/(2e)=2.068\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm{Wb}$: again the integer is a winding and the quantum is a separate input. \textbf{[0SM reading]} Charge has the same structure: the integer is the winding number of Table~\ref{tab:charge}, and the size of one quantum is a separate input that the model does not yet supply.

It also matters which number is to be derived. Since 2019 the elementary charge $e$ is a defined constant of the SI~\cite{ref:si}. The physical, unit-free number is the fine-structure constant
\begin{equation}
\alpha=\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c}=\frac{1}{137.036}.
\label{eq:alpha}
\end{equation}
The numerical values of the constants used in this paper are those of CODATA 2018~\cite{ref:codata}.
In a connection $D\theta=d\theta+qA$, the charge $q$ plays the role of a conversion rate between a dimensionless internal phase and the electromagnetic potential, in the same way that $\beta$ converts one internal rate into another (Sec.~\ref{sec:time}). The open problem is therefore not ``the mechanism of charge'' in general, but one number: where $\alpha$ comes from in the model.

\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.93\columnwidth}{\justifying\small \textbf{Statement of scope.} The 0-Sphere model, as developed in this paper, derives the \emph{integer} of electric charge: its existence, its quantization, its conservation, its bound $|Q|\le1$, and its sign. It does \emph{not} derive the \emph{size} of the charge. The fine-structure constant $\alpha=1/137.036$ is not derived here, and neither is the coupling of the winding number to the electromagnetic potential.}}
\end{center}

\misread{``The charge must sit on one of the two kernels.''}{It does not. The winding number belongs to the curve traced by the \emph{pair} of amplitudes, not to either amplitude separately; neither kernel alone has a winding number, just as neither of two hands alone has a handshake. Assigning charge to a single term of the identity, such as the transit term~\cite{ref:p42}, gives a quantity that oscillates in time and so is not conserved, and it gives detuned pairs more ``charge'' than locked ones. The integer is global.}

\established{Closing the path around the origin of the amplitude plane gives an integer. For locked kernels this integer is the protected winding number $W=\pm1$, conserved, bounded by one and carrying a sign; for detuned kernels nothing is protected. The model identifies the protected integer with charge and does not derive its size.}

\section{The Quartic Energy as a Pullback of Quadratic Bloch Forms}
\label{sec:quartic}

\paragraph{Motivation.} Relativistic quantum mechanics says that energy density is quadratic in the amplitude, of the form $\psi^\dagger H\psi$, whereas the model's kernel energies are fourth powers of the amplitude. This section shows that the two are compatible: the fourth power is a quadratic energy seen through the double cover. The section keeps the results of the earlier draft of this paper and corrects its reading of temperature.

\subsection{The kernel rotor and its first-order equation}

Collect the two amplitudes into a real two-component object, the \emph{kernel rotor},
\begin{equation}
\chi(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\tfrac{\omega t}{2}\\[3pt]\sin\tfrac{\omega t}{2}\end{pmatrix},\qquad \chi^\dagger\chi=1 .
\label{eq:rotor}
\end{equation}
Its squared components are the occupations $n_A,n_B$, and it returns to itself only after $\omega t=4\pi$, since $\chi(t+2\pi/\omega)=-\chi(t)$. Differentiating once,
\begin{equation}
\dot\chi=\frac{\omega}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\chi
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
i\,\partial_t\chi=H\chi,\qquad H=\frac{\omega}{2}\sigma_y ,
\label{eq:foe}
\end{equation}
in units $\hbar=1$. The generator is Hermitian with eigenvalues $\pm\omega/2$ and squares to a multiple of the identity,
\begin{equation}
H^2=\left(\frac{\omega}{2}\right)^{2}\openone \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (i\partial_t)^2\chi=\left(\frac{\omega}{2}\right)^{2}\chi .
\label{eq:kgroot}
\end{equation}
\textbf{[standard]} The free Dirac equation~\cite{ref:dirac28} at rest reads $i\hbar\partial_t\psi=\beta_D mc^2\psi$ with $\beta_D^2=\openone$, so its square is the Klein--Gordon equation~\cite{ref:klein,ref:gordon26} at rest. Equation~\eqref{eq:kgroot} is this square-root relation for a particle whose rest energy is $\hbar\omega/2$. Setting
\begin{equation}
\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}=mc^2
\label{eq:idmass}
\end{equation}
makes the eigenvalues of $H$ equal to $\pm mc^2$, the Dirac positive- and negative-energy rest energies, which Ref.~\cite{ref:p7} identified with the two kernels.

\subsection{Unitary equivalence to the Dirac rest solutions}

The eigenvectors of $H$ are $u_\pm=(1,\pm i)^\top/\sqrt2$ with eigenvalues $\pm\omega/2$. Projecting and evolving gives the exact decomposition
\begin{equation}
\chi(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Big[e^{-i\omega t/2}u_{+}+e^{+i\omega t/2}u_{-}\Big],
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
whose upper and lower components are $\cos(\omega t/2)$ and $\sin(\omega t/2)$.

\paragraph{The decomposition in steps.} (i) The eigenvectors: $H u=\lambda u$ with $H=\tfrac{\omega}{2}\sigma_y$ gives $\lambda=\pm\omega/2$ and $u_\pm=(1,\pm i)^\top/\sqrt2$. (ii) The coefficients at $t=0$: $c_\pm=u_\pm^\dagger\chi(0)=u_\pm^\dagger(1,0)^\top=1/\sqrt2$. (iii) Each eigenvector evolves by its own phase, $e^{\mp i\omega t/2}$. (iv) Adding the two, the upper component is $\tfrac12(e^{-i\omega t/2}+e^{i\omega t/2})=\cos(\omega t/2)$ and the lower is $\tfrac{i}{2}(e^{-i\omega t/2}-e^{i\omega t/2})=\sin(\omega t/2)$, which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:rotor}. No approximation enters at any step.

\begin{theorem}[Rest-frame equivalence]
\label{thm:equiv}
The kernel rotor $\chi(t)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:rotor} is unitarily equivalent, through the fixed unitary that diagonalizes $H=\tfrac{\omega}{2}\sigma_y$, to the equal-weight superposition $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\psi^{+}+\psi^{-})$ of the Dirac positive- and negative-energy rest solutions, provided $\hbar\omega/2=mc^2$. The kernel basis and the Dirac energy basis are one Hilbert space in two frames.
\end{theorem}

Three consequences follow. The two branch weights are equal and constant, $|c_\pm|^2=\tfrac12$; what oscillates in the kernel frame is pure relative phase in the energy frame. The mean energy vanishes, $\langle H\rangle=0$, so the symmetric two-kernel state is the balanced $\pm$ superposition rather than a stationary free electron. And an equal superposition of the two branches is exactly the standard condition for zitterbewegung~\cite{ref:schrodinger,ref:thaller}, so the model's permanently oscillating interior and the standard condition for trembling motion coincide.

\paragraph{The same state in two frames.} Figure~\ref{fig:frames} draws what Theorem~\ref{thm:equiv} says. In the kernel frame the two occupations swing between $0$ and $1$; in the energy frame the two branch weights stay at $\tfrac12$. Nothing is lost in the change of frame: the swinging of the occupations is carried, in the energy frame, entirely by the relative phase $e^{-i\omega t}$ between the two branches. This is why the theorem can call the kernel picture and the Dirac picture ``one Hilbert space in two frames.''

\subsection{Zitterbewegung as the interference of the two branches}
\label{sec:interf}

\paragraph{The exchange is the interference term.} Substituting the decomposition~\eqref{eq:decomp} into the occupation of kernel $A$, $n_A=|\chi_1|^2$, separates what each branch contributes alone from what the two contribute together,
\begin{equation}
n_A=\underbrace{\tfrac14+\tfrac14}_{\text{each branch alone}}+\underbrace{\tfrac12\cos\omega t}_{\text{cross term}},
\label{eq:interf}
\end{equation}
and $n_B=\tfrac14+\tfrac14-\tfrac12\cos\omega t$. The separate weights of the two branches are constant. Everything in the interior that changes in time is built from the cross term alone: the occupations, the energy flow $\dot n_A=-\dot n_B=-\tfrac{\omega}{2}\sin\omega t$, and the transit energy $K=\tfrac12E_0\sin^2\omega t$. \textbf{[standard]} In Dirac theory the cross term between positive- and negative-energy solutions is the zitterbewegung~\cite{ref:schrodinger,ref:thaller}. \textbf{[0SM reading]} The energy exchange between the two kernels is that cross term, read as a real oscillation of the electron's interior rather than as an artefact of the representation.

\paragraph{Two readings of negative energy.} \textbf{[standard]} The negative-energy solutions arise because the Dirac equation is a square root of $E^2=p^2c^2+m^2c^4$, and the square root admits both signs~\cite{ref:dirac28}. Quantum field theory reads the negative branch as the antiparticle. \textbf{[0SM reading]} The model reads the sign differently. An energy eigenvalue is an eigenvalue of $i\hbar\,\partial_t$, so its sign is the sign of a frequency: the sense in which the phase advances in time. A real oscillation such as $\cos(\omega t/2)$ contains the positive and the negative frequency with equal weight, so the real kernel amplitudes carry both branches equally within one particle, and no second particle is required. In the kernel frame the two signs register as a pair of opposite rates: while one kernel radiates, the other absorbs, $\dot n_A=-\dot n_B$ exactly. An electron with only one branch would have no cross term, hence no exchange and a frozen interior; by energy conservation the radiating side and the absorbing side must always occur together. The negative branch is therefore read not as negative energy held by anything, but as the decreasing side of a paired increase and decrease of thermal potential energy.

\paragraph{A frame statement, not a one-to-one assignment.} The pairing does not assign the positive branch to kernel $A$ and the negative branch to kernel $B$. The state of kernel $A$ alone, $(1,0)^\top=(u_++u_-)/\sqrt2$, contains both branches equally. The kernel pair $\{A,B\}$ and the branch pair $\{+,-\}$ are two bases of one two-dimensional space (Theorem~\ref{thm:equiv}); the sign pair of the energy basis appears in the kernel basis as the pair of opposite-signed rates, and the cross term between the two branches is the exchange itself.

\paragraph{What this reading must account for.} Two points separate it from the standard position. First, the equal superposition has $\langle H\rangle=0$, so the rest energy of the electron is carried by the total $E_0$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:central}, not by the mean of $H$ (see the factor of two in Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}). Second, in standard theory a wave packet built from positive-energy solutions alone shows no zitterbewegung, and the Foldy--Wouthuysen transformation removes it~\cite{ref:fw}; the model's claim is that the equal superposition is the permanent interior of the electron. The measurable consequence is the sub-luminal transport speed of Sec.~\ref{sec:exp}.

\misread{``Without antiparticles, the negative-energy solutions of the Dirac equation have no meaning.''}{In the model they keep a meaning inside one particle. The sign of an energy eigenvalue is the sign of a frequency, a real oscillation needs both, and the cross term between the two branches is exactly the energy exchange between the kernels, Eq.~\eqref{eq:interf}. The model does not deny the antiparticle; it adds a reading of the sign that does not require one.}

\subsection{The complex unit is generated by the oscillator}

\paragraph{Why this is needed.} Theorem~\ref{thm:equiv} used the imaginary unit $i$. If that $i$ had to be borrowed from Dirac theory, the equivalence would be a change of basis in standard quantum mechanics rather than a statement about the model.

\textbf{[standard]} For any harmonic oscillator, the combination $\alpha_{\mathrm{osc}}\propto x+ip/(m\omega)$ obeys $\dot\alpha_{\mathrm{osc}}=-i\omega\,\alpha_{\mathrm{osc}}$: a sinusoid is the shadow of a uniform rotation in phase space, and multiplication by $i$ is that rotation, the symplectic complex structure~\cite{ref:arnold}. \textbf{[0SM reading]} The photon sphere is driven as a harmonic oscillator by the energy gradient, with velocity and acceleration $v=\cos\omega t$, $a=-\sin\omega t$~\cite{ref:p10}. The Pauli expectation values of the rotor are
\begin{equation}
\langle\sigma_z\rangle=\cos\omega t,\qquad \langle\sigma_x\rangle=\sin\omega t ,
\label{eq:pauli}
\end{equation}
so that $(v,a)=(\langle\sigma_z\rangle,-\langle\sigma_x\rangle)$: the oscillator's velocity--acceleration pair traces the same circle as the Dirac zitterbewegung coherence vector, and the two share one complex structure. The identity is one of \emph{form}; the rates of the two rotations are discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:time}.

\subsection{The fourth power is a quadratic form on the Bloch sphere}

The Bloch vector~\cite{ref:bloch46} $\Bloch=\chi^\dagger\bm{\sigma}\chi=(\sin\omega t,0,\cos\omega t)$ is bilinear in $\chi$ and rotates at the full angle $\omega t$, whereas $\chi$ rotates at $\omega t/2$. The map $\chi\mapsto\Bloch$ is two-to-one: it is the Hopf map~\cite{ref:hopf}, the double cover $SU(2)\to SO(3)$. Using $n_{A,B}=(1\pm s_z)/2$ and $\sin^2\omega t=s_x^2$,
\begin{align}
T_A&=E_0n_A^2=\tfrac14E_0(1+s_z)^2, \label{eq:TAq}\\
T_B&=E_0n_B^2=\tfrac14E_0(1-s_z)^2, \label{eq:TBq}\\
K&=\tfrac12E_0\sin^2\omega t=\tfrac12E_0s_x^2 , \label{eq:Kq}
\end{align}
and the conservation law becomes $\tfrac12(1+s_z^2)+\tfrac12s_x^2=\tfrac12(1+|\Bloch|^2)=1$. Every channel is quadratic in the full-angle vector $\Bloch$.

\paragraph{The double cover drawn as a Riemann surface.} The vector $\Bloch$ is used here as bookkeeping for the kernel occupations, $s_z=n_A-n_B$, and not as a spin vector. The geometry that matters for the fourth power is the two-to-one map itself, and it is drawn without that risk as the Riemann surface of the square root, Fig.~\ref{fig:riemann}. \textbf{[standard]} The function $w=\sqrt{z}$ is two-valued; continuing it once around the branch point $z=0$ carries it from one sheet to the other, $w\to-w$, and a second circuit returns it to the first~\cite{ref:needham}. \textbf{[0SM reading]} The rotor is the square root of the full-angle phase in this sense: one turn of $\theta$ carries $\chi$ to $-\chi$, on the other sheet, and two turns return it, which is the $4\pi$ periodicity of the spinor. The Dirac energy carries the same two-sheeted structure. As a function of complex momentum, $E(p)=\sqrt{p^2c^2+m^2c^4}$ has branch points at $p=\pm imc$, and continuation around one of them exchanges the positive- and negative-energy sheets. The square root that linearizes the Klein--Gordon equation and the square root that relates the rotor to the occupations are thus two appearances of one kind of surface, which is why the Dirac equation and analytic continuation fit together naturally. A Riemann-surface reading of the two spin states was proposed early in the series~\cite{ref:p3}.

\begin{proposition}[Geometric origin of the quartic]
\label{prop:quartic}
Given (i) the integrated energy gradient $T_B-T_A=-E_0s_z$~\cite{ref:p1}, (ii) the oscillator kinetic energy $K=\tfrac12E_0s_x^2$~\cite{ref:p10}, and (iii) energy conservation, the kernel energies are uniquely $T_{A,B}=\tfrac14E_0(1\pm s_z)^2$: quadratic forms on the Bloch sphere whose pullback through $\chi\mapsto\Bloch$ is $E_0\cos^4(\omega t/2)$ and $E_0\sin^4(\omega t/2)$. No independent Stefan--Boltzmann assumption is used.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Conservation and (ii) give $T_A+T_B=E_0(1-\tfrac12s_x^2)=\tfrac12E_0(1+s_z^2)$. Together with (i), $T_A=\tfrac12[(T_A+T_B)-(T_B-T_A)]=\tfrac14E_0(1+s_z)^2$, and likewise for $T_B$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Where temperature lives (correction to the earlier draft)}

Reference~\cite{ref:p1} motivated the fourth powers by the Stefan--Boltzmann law~\cite{ref:stefan,ref:boltzmann}, taking the kernel temperature proportional to $\cos(\theta/2)$. The earlier draft of this paper kept that reading as a ``dual'' description. We withdraw it. A temperature proportional to the amplitude $\cos(\theta/2)$ would turn negative for $\theta>\pi$ and would change sign under the unobservable flip $\chi\to-\chi$. Temperature belongs to the occupation layer of Sec.~\ref{sec:conv}, $\mathcal{T}_A\propto n_A$, and then the kernel energy is the \emph{square} of temperature, $T_A=E_0n_A^2$, the scaling of black-body radiation in one spatial dimension. The fourth power is a property of the amplitude, not of temperature. Proposition~\ref{prop:quartic} shows where it comes from: the double cover acting on a quadratic energy.

\subsection{Degree matching with the Dirac equation}

\paragraph{Motivation.} The Dirac equation in three dimensions has four solutions, and the fermionic identity is of fourth degree. The two counts are compared here factor by factor.

\textbf{[standard]} The number of solutions is the degree of the characteristic polynomial. For the $4\times4$ Dirac Hamiltonian in $3+1$ dimensions,
\begin{equation}
\det\!\big(E-H_D\big)=\big(E^2-E_p^2\big)^2,\qquad E_p^2=p^2c^2+m^2c^4 ,
\label{eq:charpoly}
\end{equation}
the square of the Klein--Gordon polynomial: two distinct roots $\pm E_p$, each twice degenerate. In $1+1$ dimensions the Dirac matrices are $2\times2$ and the polynomial is $E^2-E_p^2$ itself, with two roots. The four roots therefore factor as $2\times2$. The first factor, the sign pair $\pm E_p$, comes from the square root of $E^2=p^2c^2+m^2c^4$, that is, from squaring the mass, and is present in every dimension. The second factor, the spin degeneracy, needs three spatial dimensions.

\textbf{[0SM reading]} The first factor is not a coincidence. The sign pair is the pair on which the model is built: the kernel pair and the branch pair are two bases of one space (Theorem~\ref{thm:equiv}), the exchange between the kernels is the interference of the two branches (Eq.~\eqref{eq:interf}), and both square roots are sheets of one kind of Riemann surface (Fig.~\ref{fig:riemann}). The second factor remains open. It has a home in the model only if the internal angle can be identified with a spatial rotation angle, or if spin is carried by a separate degree of freedom (Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}). The match ``fourth degree, four roots'' is therefore half structural and half open.

\subsection{Relation to the square-root construction on $S^3$}
\label{sec:rel64}

A companion paper~\cite{ref:p64} builds the electron's spin two-valuedness from the square root of the Laplacian on the three-sphere $S^3\cong SU(2)$. There the Dirac operator obeys the Lichnerowicz identity~\cite{ref:lichnerowicz} $\mathcal{D}^2=-\Delta+R/4$ with $R=6$ on the unit sphere, its eigenvalues come in signed pairs $\pm(\ell+\tfrac32)$, and the obstruction to choosing one sign globally is the double cover $SU(2)\to SO(3)$. That construction is operator-theoretic and spatial. By its own account it uses no complex structure, no Bloch vector and no derivation of the quartic energy, and its Appendix~C leaves open whether the square-root signature of the energy identity is ``more than formal.'' The present section answers that question in the affirmative: the square root appears here as the first-order evolution~\eqref{eq:foe} of the internal clock, the complex unit is the oscillator's symplectic structure, and the quartic is derived (Proposition~\ref{prop:quartic}). The two papers realize the same double cover at two ends, an elliptic operator on a spatial sphere and a first-order evolution in internal time, which a Wick-rotation bridge~\cite{ref:wick} would have to join; we do not construct that bridge. The rotor is at each instant a definite point on the Bloch sphere, so the equal $\pm$ superposition of Theorem~\ref{thm:equiv} is a deterministic rotation rather than a probabilistic mixture, consistent with the time-sequential reading of Ref.~\cite{ref:p64}.

\established{The rotor behind the identity is a square root of Klein--Gordon at rest and is unitarily equivalent to an equal superposition of the Dirac positive- and negative-energy solutions. The complex unit is the oscillator's own. The fourth powers are quadratic Bloch energies pulled back through the double cover; temperature lives on the occupation layer. The kernel exchange is the interference of the two Dirac branches, so the sign factor of the degree match with the Dirac equation is structural; the spin factor remains open.}

\section{Internal Time: The Transport Cycle}
\label{sec:time}

\paragraph{Motivation.} The angle $\theta$ advances, and the clock against which it advances has to be specified. Standard quantum mechanics uses an external time $t$ shared by every particle in the universe. That shared time is precisely a background. A model that wants to be background independent must let each particle carry its own clock, and must say how fast it runs.

\subsection{The half-angle from kinematics}

\textbf{[0SM reading]} Reference~\cite{ref:p10} gives the half-angle a kinematic origin. With velocity $v=\cos\omega t$ and acceleration $a=-\sin\omega t$, the Thomas precession rate~\cite{ref:thomas26,ref:thomas27} is
\begin{equation}
\Omega=\frac{1}{2c^2}\,(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{v})\;\propto\;-\tfrac12\sin2\omega t .
\label{eq:thomas}
\end{equation}
The oscillation has phase $\omega t$, the precession has phase $2\omega t$. Writing the rotation angle as $\theta=2\omega t$, the time phase is exactly $\theta/2$. The ratio two found here from kinematics is the same ratio that the identity requires algebraically: in $\cos^4\phi+\sin^4\phi+\tfrac12\sin^2(k\phi)=1$ only $k=2$ works.

\paragraph{The computation in steps.} Equation~\eqref{eq:thomas} uses only the product of the two sinusoids. With $v=\cos\omega t$ and $a=-\sin\omega t$, and with the precession axis normal to the plane of the motion, the magnitude is $\tfrac{1}{2c^2}(-\sin\omega t)(\cos\omega t)=-\tfrac{1}{4c^2}\sin2\omega t$, by $2\sin x\cos x=\sin2x$. (Reference~\cite{ref:p10} writes $\Omega$ as a scalar; the unit vector normal to the plane is supplied in Ref.~\cite{ref:p19}.) The doubling of the frequency is therefore a consequence of multiplying two sinusoids of the same frequency, nothing more and nothing less.

\subsection{The anomalous magnetic moment fixes the rate}

Reference~\cite{ref:p10} reads the anomalous magnetic moment as the Lorentz contraction of the half-turn, with the root-mean-square average of a harmonic oscillation,
\begin{align}
\sqrt{1-\beta^2}&=\frac{1}{1+a_e/\sqrt2},
\label{eq:beta}\\
a_e&=0.001\,159\,652\,180\,59(13),
\notag
\end{align}
which gives $\beta^2=0.0016379809$ and $\beta=0.0404720$, with $a_e$ the measured value of Ref.~\cite{ref:fan23}. The form without the root-mean-square factor, the bridge equation $\gamma=1+a_e$~\cite{ref:p15,ref:p62}, gives the peak value $0.04812$; the geometric origin of the factor $1/\sqrt2$ is discussed in Ref.~\cite{ref:p62}; the internal time uses the averaged rate. No parameter is adjusted: $\beta$ follows from the measured $a_e$ alone, and the resulting transport speed $\beta c\approx0.04047\,c$ is the model's principal unmeasured prediction. In standard theory $a_e$ is computed in quantum electrodynamics, beginning with Schwinger's $\alpha/2\pi$~\cite{ref:schwinger} and now to tenth order~\cite{ref:aoyama}; the model offers a different reading of the same measured number, not a competing calculation of it.

\paragraph{From $a_e$ to $\beta$ in four steps.} (i) Divide by $\sqrt2$: $a_e/\sqrt2=8.1999792\times10^{-4}$. (ii) Form the contraction factor: $1/(1+a_e/\sqrt2)=0.99918067$. (iii) Square and subtract from one: $\beta^2=1-0.99836202=0.0016379809$. (iv) Take the root: $\beta=0.0404720$, so $\beta c=1.2133\times10^{7}$ m/s. Every input is the measured $a_e$ and the definition of $c$.

\misread{``$0.04\,c$ is the speed of the electron.''}{It is the speed of the internal transfer of energy between the two kernels, inside an electron that may be at rest as a whole. It is the analogue of the speed of a piston inside an engine, not the speed of the car. Dirac theory assigns the corresponding internal motion the speed of light, $\pm c$; the model's sub-luminal value is what distinguishes it experimentally (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp}).}

\begin{equation}

Writing the tick as a map rather than as a phase difference $q_B-q_A$ is deliberate: a difference presupposes that the two states are read on a common scale, and supplying that scale is exactly what the transport does. The statement ``time stops when radiation stops'' of the series is, in this language, the statement that no tick occurs when no transfer is completed.

\subsection{Proper time and background independence}

\paragraph{Why this choice fits general relativity.} The transport rate $\beta mc^2/\hbar$ is the rest energy times a pure number fixed by $a_e$. \textbf{[standard]} The phase of a free particle advances as $mc^2\tau/\hbar$, with $\tau$ its proper time; this Compton clock has been read out directly with atom interferometry~\cite{ref:lan13}. \textbf{[0SM reading]} The internal time therefore advances as $\beta mc^2\tau/\hbar$: it is proportional to proper time, it is carried by the particle rather than by a background, in the spirit of relational accounts of time~\cite{ref:pagewootters,ref:rovelli}, and it slows in a gravitational potential exactly as every atomic clock does. This last property is not optional. Local position invariance~\cite{ref:will14} has been tested with optical clocks at the level of height differences of a few tens of centimetres~\cite{ref:chou10}; a clock whose rate depended on the particle species in a gravitational field would be excluded. Because $\beta$ is a constant, the transport clock passes this test automatically. The path along which the transfer runs is the thermal geodesic of Refs.~\cite{ref:p24,ref:p51}, with optical metric $n^2(T)g$~\cite{ref:gordon23}, so the clock is also tied to geometry in the way general relativity requires.

\paragraph{A worked example: the clock one metre higher.} \textbf{[standard]} Near the surface of the Earth, a clock raised by a height $h$ runs faster by the fraction $gh/c^2$, first measured in the laboratory with gamma rays~\cite{ref:pound}; for $h=1$ m this is $1.09\times10^{-16}$, and optical clocks resolve it~\cite{ref:chou10}. \textbf{[0SM reading]} If the internal time is proportional to proper time, the transport frequency of an electron one metre higher is larger by the same fraction: $5.0007\times10^{18}\,\mathrm{Hz}\times1.09\times10^{-16}\approx546$ Hz. The point is not the number but its universality: every electron, every atom and every optical clock shifts by the same fraction, because $\beta$ is a pure number. A transport clock whose shift depended on the species would contradict the observed universality of gravitational redshift. The gravitational redshift of the electron's internal clock was first discussed in the series in Ref.~\cite{ref:p22}; the transport speed used there has since been superseded by that of Ref.~\cite{ref:p10}, which does not affect a ratio of clock rates.

\subsection{Distance from the clock}

\paragraph{Motivation.} So far nothing has had a length, so distance has to be built from something else. \textbf{[standard]} In the SI, since 1983 the metre has been defined from the second and the speed of light~\cite{ref:si}: length is not an independent standard but is built from time. \textbf{[0SM reading]} The model builds the kernel separation in the same way. Rewriting $\nu_{\mathrm{tr}}=\beta c/\lambda_C$,
\begin{equation}
\lambda_C=\beta c\times T_{\mathrm{tr}},\qquad T_{\mathrm{tr}}=\frac{1}{\nu_{\mathrm{tr}}}=1.9997\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm{s},
\label{eq:distance}
\end{equation}
which reproduces $\lambda_C=2.4263\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{m}$. As an equation this restates the definition in Ref.~\cite{ref:p10}; as a reading it says that the distance between the kernels is the transport speed times one tick of the internal clock. Distance is made from the clock. Building spacetime geometry from clocks and light signals alone is an old programme of general relativity~\cite{ref:marzke}; here the clock is internal to the particle.

\section{Where the Metric Enters: Comparing Two Clocks}
\label{sec:metric}

\paragraph{Motivation.} If every electron carries its own clock, the readings of two clocks still have to be compared. A clock in each particle does not by itself complete background independence: one must also say how the reading of one clock is carried to another. This section is an outlook. It states what the model has, what it needs, and where each missing piece enters; it does not close the problem.

\subsection{Two of Maxwell's equations need no metric}

\textbf{[standard]} In the language of differential forms, Maxwell's equations split into
\begin{align}
d\FF&=0, && \text{(Faraday, no monopoles)} \label{eq:dF}\\
d\mathbf{G}&=\mathbf{J}, && \text{(Gauss, Amp\`ere)} \label{eq:dG}\\
\mathbf{G}&=\hodge\FF. && \text{(constitutive relation)} \label{eq:constit}
\end{align}
Here $\FF=(\mathbf{E},\mathbf{B})$ is the field strength and $\mathbf{G}=(\mathbf{D},\mathbf{H})$ the excitation. Equations~\eqref{eq:dF} and~\eqref{eq:dG} use only the exterior derivative and need no metric; the metric, and the vacuum constants with it, enter only through the Hodge star in Eq.~\eqref{eq:constit}~\cite{ref:hehl}. This metric-free reading of electrodynamics goes back to Kottler, Cartan and van Dantzig~\cite{ref:kottler,ref:cartan23,ref:vandantzig}. The structural overview~\cite{ref:p68} makes the metric-free sector the starting point of the model, and Ref.~\cite{ref:p54} derives Maxwell electrodynamics as the low-energy limit of the model's line-integral ontology. We add one refinement: Eq.~\eqref{eq:dG} needs only the \emph{conservation} of charge, $d\mathbf{J}=0$, for $\mathbf{G}$ to exist, not a mechanism for charge. The model supplies conservation (Table~\ref{tab:charge}). If $\FF$ is the curvature of a connection, Eq.~\eqref{eq:dF} holds automatically.

\subsection{Comparing two electrons}

Keep the four kernels apart: electron 1 has kernels $A,B$; electron 2 has kernels $A',B'$. The comparison of their clocks is a map of the same type as the tick, Eq.~\eqref{eq:tick}, but at a different level,
\begin{equation}
\Tmap_{(AB)\to(A'B')}:\ q_{AB}\longmapsto q_{A'B'}^{\,\mathrm{comp}},
\label{eq:compare}
\end{equation}
where $q_{A'B'}^{\,\mathrm{comp}}$ is the state of electron 2 assigned by comparison with electron 1, not the state of electron 2 itself. The path of the comparison is the one selected by least action, the thermal geodesic. The idea that a $U(1)$ connection is a rule for synchronizing internal clocks goes back in the series to Ref.~\cite{ref:p17}, and the gauge principle itself, as a rule for comparing phases at different points, to Weyl~\cite{ref:weyl29}; here it is applied between electrons rather than assumed as a field. Mathematically such a map is a parallel transport~\cite{ref:nakahara}; if the states are phases on a circle, it is a rotation $e^{ia_{12}}$. Two outcomes are then possible. If composing comparisons around every closed chain of electrons, $\Tmap_{3\to1}\circ\Tmap_{2\to3}\circ\Tmap_{1\to2}$, returns the identity, the comparisons are a pure gradient and carry no field. Products of phases around closed loops are the Wilson loops of lattice gauge theory~\cite{ref:wilson74}, and the non-integrable phase factor is the complete description of electromagnetism in the sense of Wu and Yang~\cite{ref:wuyang}. If some chain does not return the identity, the comparisons have curvature: this is the rotational part of the Helmholtz decomposition~\eqref{eq:helmholtz}, and it is the candidate for $\FF=(\mathbf{E},\mathbf{B})$. Inside one electron the domain is contractible and every curl-free comparison is a gradient, so a single electron produces no holonomy; holonomy needs at least three electrons forming a loop. A continuous field $A_\mu$ is not assumed at the start: if it appears, it appears as the description of these comparisons once they fill a surface and a volume.

\paragraph{A worked example of holonomy.} Suppose the comparison maps between three electrons are rotations of the clock phase by $a_{12}$, $a_{23}$ and $a_{31}$. Going round the triangle multiplies the three rotations, so the holonomy is the phase $a_{12}+a_{23}+a_{31}$ modulo $2\pi$. With $a_{12}=0.3$, $a_{23}=0.5$, $a_{31}=-0.8$ the sum is $0$: the comparisons can be undone by resetting each clock's origin, and there is no field. With $a_{31}=-0.6$ instead, the sum is $0.2$: no choice of clock origins removes it, because resetting the origin of electron $2$ adds to $a_{12}$ exactly what it subtracts from $a_{23}$. That residue is gauge-invariant; it is the discrete form of $\oint\mathcal{A}=\int\FF$, the flux through the triangle.

\paragraph{The standard example: Aharonov--Bohm.} \textbf{[standard]} Electrons sent around a long solenoid travel where the magnetic field is zero, yet the interference pattern shifts by the phase $e\Phi/\hbar$~\cite{ref:ab59,ref:tonomura86}, where $\Phi$ is the flux inside the solenoid (Fig.~\ref{fig:ab}). The observable is the holonomy around the loop, not a local field on the paths. The series has used this as the paradigm that line integrals, not local fields, are the primary observables~\cite{ref:p29,ref:p30,ref:p31}, and Ref.~\cite{ref:p63} maps the route from line integrals to covariant derivatives; the comparison maps of Eq.~\eqref{eq:compare} are the same idea applied between electrons.

\section{Distance from Standard Theory}
\label{sec:limits}

We collect the limits of the paper in one place, so that the main text could move forward without a caveat in every paragraph.

\paragraph{Rest frame only.} Theorem~\ref{thm:equiv} and Proposition~\ref{prop:quartic} describe the zero-momentum sector. The kinetic term $c\,\bm{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{p}$, Lorentz covariance and the Clifford algebra are not derived.

\paragraph{Sign sector, not spin sector.} The equivalence is two-dimensional and lives in the $\pm$ energy subspace at fixed spin. The spin degeneracy of the Dirac four-spinor is a second two-valued quantity, independent of the first; where it sits in the model is open. In particular the amplitude sign $\chi\to-\chi$ cannot be the spin-up/spin-down pair, because $\chi$ and $-\chi$ are the same physical state, while spin up and spin down are orthogonal states. In the language of the Hopf fibration, the sign lives in the fibre and the spin direction in the base. The sign factor of the degree match of Eq.~\eqref{eq:charpoly} is structural (Sec.~\ref{sec:quartic}); the spin factor becomes structural only if the internal angle can be identified with a spatial rotation angle.

\paragraph{Charge and spin.} Section~\ref{sec:winding} identifies the sense in which the amplitude $z$ winds with the sign of charge. If spin is also carried by circulation, namely by the photon sphere in transit, the two quantities compete for one orientation. Either they are separate degrees of freedom, with charge the winding in the amplitude plane and spin the angular momentum of the transported photon sphere in space, or they share one orientation, and then the model predicts a correlation between the sign of charge and the sense of spin that must be stated and tested. This paper does not decide between the two.

\paragraph{A residual factor of two.} With Eq.~\eqref{eq:idmass} the branches carry $\pm mc^2$ and the gap is $\hbar\omega=2mc^2$. Whether $E_0$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:central} is $mc^2$ or $2mc^2$ is an open bookkeeping question; Refs.~\cite{ref:p38,ref:p43,ref:p46} make different choices.

\paragraph{The rate of the kinematic oscillation.} Section~\ref{sec:quartic} matches the form of the oscillator's motion with the Dirac coherence vector; Sec.~\ref{sec:time} assigns the transport its own rate, $\beta$ times slower. The two statements are compatible only as a statement about form plus a ledger of rates, Table~\ref{tab:ledger}. Grounding the internal time in Ref.~\cite{ref:p10} adopts that paper's velocity convention, $v=\cos\omega t$.

\paragraph{Ontology.} That two sinusoids are shadows of one rotation is a mathematical identity. It does not by itself establish that the internal oscillation is real; the standard position, that rest-frame trembling is a representation artifact removed by the Foldy--Wouthuysen transformation~\cite{ref:fw}, remains available. The model's falsifiable separation from that position is the sub-luminal transport speed $\beta c\approx0.04047\,c$, which contradicts the $\pm c$ eigenvalues of the Dirac velocity operator; Sec.~\ref{sec:exp} lists it with the other experimental handles.

\paragraph{Integers, not sizes.} We repeat the statement of scope of Sec.~\ref{sec:winding} without qualification. The model derives the existence, integer value, conservation, bound and sign of charge (Appendix~\ref{app:charge}), and the integer structure of the two pictures. It does not derive the size of charge, the fine-structure constant $\alpha$, the coupling to the electromagnetic potential, or the light-cone structure. Each is a separate open problem, named in Sec.~\ref{sec:metric}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked where the integers of an electron model come from, and followed one thread from arithmetic to physics. The integers are fixed by $0$ and $1$; the numbers unchanged by squaring are $\{0,1\}$; the invertible integers are $\{\pm1\}=S^0$. Inside the electron, the same normalization read at degree one is solved by a segment and read at degree two, in its kernel part, only by the segment's two ends: the boson is $D^1$, the fermion's kernels are $S^0=\partial D^1$, and the cross term is the seat of transport that makes the two separated points path-connected. Closing the path into a loop around the origin of the amplitude plane gives an integer winding number, which the model identifies with charge. We state the result and its limit together: the model derives the integer of charge, its existence, quantization, conservation, bound and sign, and it does not derive the size of the charge; the fine-structure constant remains an input, exactly as in quantized vortices and flux quanta, where the integer and its quantum come from different places.

The earlier content of this paper is retained: the rotor behind the identity is a square root of Klein--Gordon at rest and is unitarily equivalent to the Dirac rest solutions; the complex unit is the oscillator's symplectic structure; and the fourth powers are quadratic Bloch forms pulled back through the Hopf double cover, with temperature now placed in the occupation layer. The internal time is the transport cycle, running at $\beta mc^2/\hbar$ with $\beta=0.04047$ fixed by the anomalous magnetic moment, exactly $\beta$ times slower than the Dirac branch phase and proportional to proper time; the distance between the kernels is the transport speed times one tick. No metric is needed while the kernels merely sit; distance, the constitutive relation and the fine-structure constant enter together when the photon sphere crosses space. The model stands or falls experimentally on the transport speed $\beta c\approx0.04\,c$, fixed by $a_e$ with no free parameter. Figure~\ref{fig:flow} summarises the flow.

\section{The Bloch Map and the Derivation of the Quartic}
\label{app:bloch}

\paragraph{Half-angle to full-angle.} For the rotor~\eqref{eq:rotor}, the occupations are $n_{A,B}=\tfrac12(1\pm\cos\omega t)$ by the half-angle formulas, so $s_z=n_A-n_B=\cos\omega t$ and, from $2\cos\phi\sin\phi=\sin2\phi$, $s_x=\sin\omega t$; $s_y=0$ because $\chi$ is real. The rotor advances by $\omega t/2$, the Bloch vector by $\omega t$: this is the two-to-one map.

\paragraph{Uniqueness.} The two equations of the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:quartic}, the sum fixed by conservation and the oscillator energy and the difference fixed by the energy gradient, determine $T_A$ and $T_B$ separately. The coefficient structure $\tfrac14(1\pm s_z)^2$ shows that the fourth power in $\chi$ is the square of an occupation that is linear in $s_z$.

\paragraph{The boson in the same language.} Squaring the bosonic identity~\eqref{eq:boson} at the half angle reproduces the fermionic identity, with cross term $2\sin^2(\theta/2)\cos^2(\theta/2)=\tfrac12\sin^2\theta$. The fourth power and the transit seat are what the second squaring adds; the sign flip under $\theta\to\theta+2\pi$ is already present at the first.

\section{Words That Split in Two}
\label{app:words}

Several words borrowed from established fields carry two meanings in this paper. When a reader's objection seems to miss the point, the first thing to check is whether the same word is being read in the other sense.

\paragraph{Occupation number.} Here: the share $n_A=\cos^2(\theta/2)$ of the energy held by kernel $A$, a continuous quantity. In standard theory: the eigenvalue $0$ or $1$ of the fermion number operator. Both satisfy the idempotent equation~\eqref{eq:idem} at the ends, but they are different objects.

\paragraph{Proper time.} Here: the internal clock carried by the particle, the transport cycle. In general relativity: the length of a worldline given by the metric. Section~\ref{sec:time} argues that the first runs proportionally to the second, which is a claim, not a definition.

\paragraph{Radiation.} Here: the transfer of energy between the two kernels. In electromagnetism: emission of electromagnetic waves to infinity. ``Time stops when radiation stops'' refers to the first.

\paragraph{Double cover.} Here: the sign flip of the real amplitude after one turn. In standard theory also: the covering $SU(2)\to SO(3)$ of spatial rotations. Whether the two coincide is the open question of Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}.

\paragraph{Hidden variable.} Here: a degree of freedom absent from the standard formalism, carried by the particle, as density is carried by a particle in smoothed-particle hydrodynamics~\cite{ref:monaghan}. In the Bell literature~\cite{ref:bell66}: a local variable meant to explain two-particle correlations. This paper uses only the first sense.

\section{Derivation: Charge as a Protected Winding Number}
\label{app:charge}

This appendix derives the five rows of Table~\ref{tab:charge} marked \emph{derived}. Everything here is elementary: trigonometric identities, the winding number of a closed curve, and Abel's theorem for Wronskians. No metric and no coupling constant is used, which is why the size of the charge cannot come out of it.

\subsection{The amplitude plane and the winding number}

Let $a(t)$ and $b(t)$ be the amplitudes of kernels $A$ and $B$, and write $z=a+ib$. The occupations are $n_A=a^2$, $n_B=b^2$, so $|z|^2=n_A+n_B$. \textbf{[standard]} If a closed curve $z(t)$, $0\le t\le T$, avoids the origin, its winding number~\cite{ref:hatcher}
\begin{equation}
W=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint d\arg z=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^T\frac{a\,\dot b-b\,\dot a}{a^2+b^2}\,dt
\label{eq:Wdef}
\end{equation}
is an integer, and it cannot change under any continuous deformation of the curve that keeps away from the origin. It can change only when the curve passes through the origin, that is, when both kernels are empty at the same instant. This is why two kernels are \emph{necessary}: a single amplitude passes through zero twice per cycle~\cite{ref:p1,ref:p20}.

\subsection{Normalization forces lock and selects two orientations}

Consider two kernels at the same frequency with general amplitudes and relative phase, $a=A\cos t$, $b=B\sin(t+\delta)$. A direct expansion gives
\begin{align}
a^2+b^2={}&\tfrac12(A^2+B^2)+\tfrac12\big(A^2-B^2\cos2\delta\big)\cos2t
\notag\\
&+\tfrac12B^2\sin2\delta\,\sin2t .
\label{eq:normexp}
\end{align}
The normalization $n_A+n_B=1$ at every instant requires both oscillating coefficients to vanish: $\sin2\delta=0$ and $A^2=B^2\cos2\delta$. With $B\neq0$ the second condition excludes $\cos2\delta=-1$, so
\begin{equation}
A=B=1,\qquad \delta\in\{0,\pi\}.
\label{eq:twosol}
\end{equation}
For $\delta=0$, $z=e^{it}$ and $W=+1$; for $\delta=\pi$, $z=e^{-it}$ and $W=-1$. The identity therefore has exactly two locked solutions, distinguished by orientation; the model reads them as $e^{-}$ and $e^{+}$. Any intermediate $\delta$ gives an ellipse with $|z|<1$ somewhere, and $\delta=\pi/2$ collapses the curve to the segment $a=b$ through the origin (Fig.~\ref{fig:winding}c).

For $A=B=1$ the oscillating part of Eq.~\eqref{eq:normexp} is $\tfrac12(1-\cos2\delta)\cos2t+\tfrac12\sin2\delta\sin2t$, whose amplitude is $\tfrac12\sqrt{(1-\cos2\delta)^2+\sin^22\delta}=\tfrac12\sqrt{2-2\cos2\delta}=|\sin\delta|$. Hence $\min_t|z|^2=1-|\sin\delta|$, which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:gap}: the gap closes continuously as $\delta$ moves from $0$ to $\pi/2$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:delta}). Two kernels running at different frequencies cannot satisfy the normalization at all, since the cross term then oscillates at the beat frequency. Normalization is therefore equivalent to lock.

\subsection{The Wronskian: conservation, quantization, bound}

Define, for a pair at internal angular frequency $\omega$,
\begin{equation}
Q=\frac{2}{\omega}\big(a\,\dot b-b\,\dot a\big).
\label{eq:Qapp}
\end{equation}
\paragraph{Conservation.} If $a$ and $b$ oscillate at angular frequencies $\omega_A$ and $\omega_B$, so that $\ddot a=-\omega_A^2a$ and $\ddot b=-\omega_B^2b$, then
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dt}\big(a\dot b-b\dot a\big)=a\ddot b-b\ddot a=\big(\omega_A^2-\omega_B^2\big)\,ab .
\label{eq:dQ}
\end{equation}
For locked kernels the right-hand side vanishes identically: the Wronskian of two solutions of the same linear second-order equation is constant (Abel)~\cite{ref:coddington}. For detuned kernels it does not.
\paragraph{Quantization.} For the locked rotor $a=\cos(\omega t/2)$, $b=\pm\sin(\omega t/2)$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:Qapp} gives $Q=\pm1$ exactly.
\paragraph{Bound.} By the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality $|a\dot b-b\dot a|\le|z|\,|\dot z|$. On the normalized circle $|z|=1$ and $|\dot z|=\omega/2$, so $|Q|\le1$, with equality only when $\dot z\perp z$, that is, for circular motion. There is no doubly charged locked pair.
\paragraph{Winding.} Comparing Eqs.~\eqref{eq:Wdef} and~\eqref{eq:Qapp}, the Wronskian is the density of the winding number: on the unit circle, $W$ is the time integral of $(\omega/2)Q$ divided by $2\pi$. Charge as an integer and charge as a conserved current are the same quantity, integrated or not.

\subsection{Detuned kernels: the mod-4 character}

For coprime frequency ratios $p:q$, take the curve $z(\tau)=\cos p\tau+i\sin q\tau$, $0\le\tau\le2\pi$. If $q$ is even, the curve passes through the origin and $W$ is undefined. Otherwise the curve crosses the imaginary axis at $\tau_k=\pi(2k+1)/(2p)$, $k=0,\dots,2p-1$, in the direction $(-1)^k$ and on the side $\operatorname{sign}\sin q\tau_k$; counting signed crossings of the positive imaginary axis gives
\begin{equation}
W=\tfrac12\sum_{k=0}^{2p-1}(-1)^k\,\operatorname{sign}\sin\frac{\pi q(2k+1)}{2p}.
\label{eq:Wsum}
\end{equation}
Write $n=2k+1$ and substitute $n\equiv qm\pmod{4p}$; for odd $q$ coprime to $p$ this permutes the odd residues. With the mod-4 Dirichlet character~\cite{ref:apostol} $\chi_4(m)=0,+1,-1$ for $m$ even, $m\equiv1$, $m\equiv3\pmod4$, one has $(-1)^{(m-1)/2}=\chi_4(m)$ and $\chi_4(q^{-1}n)=\chi_4(q)\chi_4(n)$, while $\operatorname{sign}\sin(\pi n/2p)$ is $+1$ for $0<n<2p$ and $-1$ for $2p<n<4p$. Hence
\begin{equation}
W=\tfrac12\chi_4(q)\Big[\sum_{\substack{n\ \mathrm{odd}\\ n<2p}}\chi_4(n)-\sum_{\substack{n\ \mathrm{odd}\\ 2p<n<4p}}\chi_4(n)\Big].
\label{eq:Wchi}
\end{equation}
For odd $p$ the first sum has $p$ alternating terms beginning with $+1$ and equals $1$; the second begins at $2p+1\equiv3\pmod4$ and equals $-1$; so $W=\chi_4(q)$. For even $p$ both sums have an even number of alternating terms and vanish, so $W=0$. The winding number of any coprime detuned pair is therefore $0$ or $\pm1$, never larger, and it is not protected: changing the relative phase moves the curve through the origin and flips its sign. Direct numerical counting agrees with Eq.~\eqref{eq:Wchi} for every coprime odd pair up to $301$. For irrational ratios the curve comes arbitrarily close to the origin and no winding number is defined. Detuned kernels carry no conserved integer: the model reads them as neutral.

\subsection{What this appendix does not show}

Nothing above fixes the size of one unit of charge or shows that the winding number couples to an electromagnetic potential. Those require a scale and a coupling, the two rows of Table~\ref{tab:charge} marked \emph{not derived}.
